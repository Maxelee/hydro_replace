\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Realizing the full scientific potential of upcoming weak gravitational lensing surveys such as Euclid \citep{euclid_collaboration_euclid_2024}, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) \citep{ivezic_lsst_2019}, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope \citep{roman} requires percent-level control of systematic uncertainties, including the impact of baryonic physics on the matter power spectrum and higher-order statistics \citep{}. This level of control is complicated by baryonic feedback from stellar winds, supernova explosions, and active galactic nuclei (AGN), which redistribute matter within and around dark matter halos \citep{}. This redistribution suppresses the matter power spectrum by 10--20\% at scales $k \sim 1\,h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ \citep{} and alters non-Gaussian statistics, such as weak lensing peak counts and the matter bispectrum \citep{}. If untreated, these effects can bias cosmological parameter constraints, particularly on $S_8 = \sigma_8(\Omega_m/0.3)^{0.5}$ by amounts exceeding statistical uncertainties from Stage-IV data \citep{}.

Hydrodynamical simulations self-consistently model gas physics, star formation, and feedback, providing the most accurate predictions of baryonic effects, but they remain computationally prohibitive for the large volumes and statistical ensembles required by modern surveys \citep{}. A single flagship hydrodynamical run, such as IllustrisTNG or FLAMINGO, requires tens of millions of CPU hours \citep{} and provides a single simulation realization at a single location in a high-dimensional subgrid model parameter space. These simulations cannot easily be repeated across the high-dimensional parameter space required to marginalize over subgrid physics effects; one would like inference pipelines \citep{}. 

This computational expense has driven the development of \emph{baryonic correction models} (BCMs), which attempt to capture baryonic effects by post-processing gravity-only dark matter simulations with analytic or semi-analytic prescriptions for gas ejection, stellar fractions, and concentration modifications \citep{}. These models can be calibrated to match the matter power spectrum suppression observed in hydrodynamical simulations \citep{} or inferred from external observables such as Sunyaev--Zel'dovich (SZ) and X-ray data \citep{}, making them powerful and physically motivated alternatives to hydrodynamical simulations.

Despite their computational efficiency, BCMs face a fundamental question of physical consistency: if a model is tuned to reproduce one statistic—for example, the 3D matter power spectrum $P(k)$—does it automatically reproduce the correct baryonic impact on other observables, such as the weak lensing convergence power spectrum $C_\ell$, peak counts, minima, or higher-order correlations \citep{}? 

Recent evidence suggests the answer is no. Comparisons of BCMs against hydrodynamical benchmarks have revealed statistic-dependent discrepancies: a BCM may match $P(k)$ at the few-percent level while failing to reproduce peak count distributions \citep{} or bispectra \citep{}. More fundamentally, \citet{} demonstrated that even a direct \emph{halo-replacement} procedure—swapping DMO halos with their matched hydrodynamical counterparts out to $2R_{200}$ in IllustrisTNG—does not fully recover the hydrodynamical matter power spectrum. This discrepancy implies that some fraction of the baryonic effect arises from lower-mass halos, larger radii, or diffuse intergalactic components not captured by simple halo-based replacement \citep{}. If even perfect halo profiles fail to reconstruct $P(k)$, then BCMs tuned to $P(k)$ must be compensating by placing baryonic modifications in regions of halo mass, radius, and redshift space that are inconsistent with hydrodymaical halos. It could then be true that these modifications propagate inconsistently to other statistics \citep{}.

This raises two critical questions. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item First, which halo masses, radii, and redshifts dominate the baryonic impact on a given observable? Understanding this attribution is essential for determining where BCMs must be most accurate and where approximations are tolerable. 
\item Second, how can we test whether a BCM is physically self-consistent across multiple observables? Matching one statistic is insufficient if the underlying density profiles of halos differ from hydrodynamical predictions in ways that matter for other statistics. 
\end{enumerate}

%Can we develop a response formalism that maps where baryonic effects originate in mass-radius space for any observable, enabling targeted BCM calibration and consistency tests: BCMs that match the response kernel of hydrodynamical simulations for multiple observables are physically self-consistent."

Answering these questions requires a unified framework that quantifies the response of arbitrary observables to baryonic modifications in specific regions of halo parameter space and provides a diagnostic for cross-statistic consistency.

In this work, we introduce a self-consistent formalism that addresses both questions as functions of mass, radius, and redshift. We define a family of hybrid ``Replace" density fields in which DMO halos at a redshift $z$ and above a mass threshold $M_{\min}$ are replaced by their hydrodynamical counterparts within a radial factor $\alpha R_{200}$, and construct a cumulative response function $F_S(M_{\min},\alpha)$ that measures the fraction of the total baryonic effect on observable $S$ captured by this replacement. By discretizing halo mass finite bins, we further define a tile response kernel $K_S(M_a,M_b;\alpha_i;z_k)$ that provides an empirical map of where baryonic effects originate in $(M,\alpha,z)$ space for each statistic. This kernel can be marginalized over mass or radius to answer questions such as: ``which halo mass range contributes most to the convergence power spectrum at $\ell=1000$?'' or ``how far in radius does one need to model correctly for peak counts at signal-to-noise $\nu=4$?'' Crucially, the formalism applies to any summary statistic—matter power spectra, weak lensing two-point functions, peak counts, minima, PDFs, and higher-order moments—enabling direct comparisons of response patterns across observables.

We apply this framework to IllustrisTNG simulations \citep{}, computing response kernels for the 3D matter power spectrum, projected 2D power spectra, weak lensing convergence power, and non-Gaussian peak statistics. We then perform baryon corrections with various BCMs \cite{} to assess whether BCMs place baryonic effects in the correct halo parameter space. A BCM that matches $K_S^{\mathrm{Hydro}}$ for multiple statistics is physically consistent. 

This paper is organized as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:formalism}, we develop the cumulative and discrete response formalisms, define the tile kernel $K_S(M_a,M_b;\alpha_i;z_k)$, and establish the mathematical properties of response fractions. In Section~\ref{sec:simulations}, we describe the IllustrisTNG DMO and Hydro runs, the halo-matching procedure, and the construction of Replace density fields. Section~\ref{sec:results_matter} presents response kernels for matter power spectra, testing additivity and quantifying the contribution of different mass--radius shells. Section~\ref{sec:results_WL} extends the analysis to weak lensing observables, including convergence power and peak counts. Section~\ref{sec:BCM} compares BCM response kernels to hydrodynamical benchmarks and discusses implications for Stage-IV survey strategies. We summarize our findings and discuss future extensions in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}.



\section{A Response Formalism}
\label{sec:formalism}
We begin with a general treatment of the response for some field level statistic to baryons. In general we can define a Replace density field as 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general_replace_density}
    \rho_{R}(\bm{x}, z) = \rho_D(\bm{x}, z)  + \sum_{i \in H} \left[\rho_H^i(\bm{x}, z) - \rho_{D}^i(\bm{x}, z)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\rho_D(\bm{x}, z)$ is the density in a dark matter only simulation at coordinate $\bm{x}$ and redshift $z$. Given some set of coordinates described by $H$, the dark matter density at that location, $\rho_D^i$ can be modified via a replacement with the hydro density, $\rho_H^i$. In the above, the framework is general such that $H$ can signify any chosen coordinates, but for this work, we will choose $H$ to be a set of halos with masses $M$ and radius factors $\alpha R_{200}$. To avoid double-counting where halo regions overlap, we assign priority by decreasing halo mass. For a given location $\bm{x}$, if multiple halos' replacement regions contain $\bm{x}$, only the most massive halo performs the replacement at that location. 
Mathematically, we define:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:replace}
\rho_R(\bm{x},z) = \rho_D(\bm{x},z) + \sum_{i \in H(M,z)} \theta_i(\bm{x}) 
\left[\rho_H^i(\bm{x},\alpha) - \rho_D^i(\bm{x},\alpha)\right],
\end{equation}

where the indicator function is
\begin{equation}
\theta_i(\bm{x}) = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } |\bm{x}-\bm{x}_i| < \alpha R_{200}^i \text{ and } i = \arg\max_j M_j 
\text{ among all } j \text{ with } |\bm{x}-\bm{x}_j| < \alpha R_{200}^j, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This ensures that each spatial point receives replacement from at most one halo.


In the limit of $M_{\rm min}$ goes to 0 or $\alpha$ becomes $\infty$, the field becomes purely hydro, and inversely, the field becomes purely dark matter only. It can then be thought of that $\rho_R$ interpolates between the DMO and hydro fields as the mass threshold and radius factor are modified. 

For any statistic that we measure from the resulting Replace field ($S_R$), we can assume that its value is somewhere between the dark matter only ($S_D$) and hydro versions ($S_H$) of the same statistic, allowing us to define some response fraction, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cum_resp_func}
    F_S(M_{\rm min}, \alpha) = \dfrac{S_R(M_{\rm min}, \alpha) - S_D}{S_H-S_D}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed $\alpha$ and redshift $z$, we can see that the fraction approaches $0$ as the minimum mass $M_{\rm min}$ approaches infinity, and 1 as it approaches 0. Or intuitively, when no mass is replaced, the fraction is 0, reflecting that the statistic extracted from the replace field is the same as in a dark matter only simulation, while when all masses are included, the field and resulting statistic is purely hydro. 

The fraction, $F_s$ is implicitly a function of a chosen statistics arguments. For example, the matter power spectrum, $P(k)$ can be computed for hydro, DMO and replace fields yielding a quantity, $F_P(M_{min}, \alpha; k)$, and which shows what masses and radius factors contribute to a given scale. It is often the case that the hydro and dmo statisticss are relativly consistent, for example at large scales in the matter power spectrum. In such a case, the fraction becomes undefined, and we must artificially fix the fraction to some constant, $F_p = c$, which we will set to 1. It should also be noted that the fraction is not necessarily bounded between $0$ and $1$. The replacement does not necessarily follow mass conservation laws, and can therefore create a statistic, $S_R$ which is no longer some smoothly interpolated location between the dmo and hydro statistic. 


The above definitions using $M_{\rm min}$ are inherently cumulative as they incorporate all halos above some threshold mass, and replacement regions in spheres according to $\alpha$. The response fraction then tells us for a given statistic, how low, and how far out do we need to go to capture the full baryonic effects on the given statistic. For example, peak counts, which are sensitive to the collapsed cores of massive halos, may show that one must only match density profiles of massive $10^{13}$ and above halos out to $0.5 \times R200$, while the power spectrum may require more. We will show in \S\ref{sec:} various statistics and how much BCMs must do to approach them. 

In the discrete version, we partition halo masses and radii into finite bins 
and define the response from a specific mass-radius tile. For a mass bin 
$[M_a, M_{a+1})$ and radius shell $[\alpha_i R_{200}, \alpha_{i+1} R_{200})$, 
we compute a Replace field with halos in that bin replaced only within the 
specified radius shell. The tile response fraction is then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disc_resp_func}
    \Delta F_S(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1}) = 
    \dfrac{S_R(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1}) - S_D}{S_H-S_D},
\end{equation}
where $S_R(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1})$ is the statistic measured from 
the Replace field with halos in the specified mass-radius bin activated. Under the assumption that contributions from different mass-radius tiles combine 
additively, we can recover the cumulative response as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive_approx}
    F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) \approx \sum_{\substack{a: M_a \geq M_{\min} \\ 
    i: \alpha_i \leq \alpha}} 
    \Delta F_S(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1}),
\end{equation}
where the sum extends over all mass bin indices $a$ with $M_a \geq M_{\min}$ 
and all radius bin indices $i$ with $\alpha_i \leq \alpha$. Deviations from 
this relation quantify the nonlinearity of the map $S[\rho]$. To diagnose the degree of nonlinearity, we define the fractional non-additivity as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:non_additivity}
    \epsilon_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) = \dfrac{F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) -
    \sum_{\substack{a: M_a \geq M_{\min} \\ i: \alpha_i \leq \alpha}} 
    \Delta F_S(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1})}{F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha)}.
\end{equation}
Values $|\epsilon_S| \ll 1$ indicate that mass-radius bins contribute approximately 
additively to the observable $S$, while larger values signal significant nonlinear 
interactions between different regions of halo parameter space.


\section{Simulations and Model Creation}

\subsection{The IllustrisTNG Suite}

We use the highest resolution version of the IllustrisTNG suite, specifically the TNG300-1 simulation alongside its dark matter only counterpart TNG300-1-Dark (henceforth TNG and TNG-DM, respectively) \citep{}. The TNG300-1 simulation spans a periodic volume with $205\,{\rm Mpc}\,h^{-1}$ on a side and evolves $2500^3$ dark matter, and $2500^3$ gas elements which can become stars and black holes from redshift $z=127$ to $z=0$.

In practice, the formalism of \S~\ref{sec:formalism} can be applied to any simulation suite containing matched initial conditions between hydrodynamical and dark matter only counterparts. We choose IllustrisTNG for this work to build upon and extend previous results \citep{miller2025} examining baryonic effects through halo replacement procedures, and previous weak lensing statistic studies such as \cite{}. Understanding the impact of different subgrid physics implementations on the response kernels derived in \S~\ref{sec:formalism} would be valuable, but we leave systematic comparison across simulation suites (e.g., FLAMINGO \citep{}, SIMBA \citep{}, EAGLE \citep{}) for future work.

Both TNG and TNG-DM adopt cosmological parameters consistent with Planck 2015 results: $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}=0.6911$, $\Omega_{m,0}=0.3089$, $\Omega_{b,0}=0.0486$, $\sigma_8=0.8159$, $n_s=0.9667$, and $h=0.6774$ \citep{}. The TNG simulation includes baryonic physics through a subgrid prescription which includes radiative cooling, star formation, stellar evolution and feedback, supermassive black hole seeding and growth, and AGN feedback \citep{}. The model was calibrated to reproduce the galaxy stellar mass function, stellar-to-halo mass relation, and cosmic star formation rate density at $z\lesssim 10$.

The mass resolution of TNG300-1 is $m_{\rm gas}=7.44\times 10^6\,M_\odot\,h^{-1}$ for initial gas cells and $m_{\rm DM}=3.98\times 10^7\,M_\odot\,h^{-1}$ for dark matter particles. The TNG-DM counterpart uses $m_{\rm DM}=4.72\times 10^7\,M_\odot\,h^{-1}$, accounting for the cosmic baryon fraction. Both simulations output particle data at 100 snapshots spanning $0 \leq z \leq 20$, with all data publicly available through the IllustrisTNG data release \citep{}.

\subsection{Halo Catalogs and Matching}

Both TNG and TNG-DM employ the Friends-of-Friends (FoF) algorithm with linking length $b=0.2$ to identify dark matter halos, followed by the SUBFIND algorithm \citep{} to locate gravitationally bound substructures within each FoF group. These catalogs are computed on-the-fly during the simulation and output at each snapshot, with particle membership indexed to enable rapid halo-to-particle association.

We construct catalogs of bijectively matched halos between TNG-DM and TNG using a particle-based matching procedure. For each halo in TNG-DM, we identify all associated dark matter particles and determine their halo membership in TNG, selecting the TNG halo containing the largest fraction of these particles as the match. We then reverse the process: starting from the matched TNG halo, we verify that it maps back to the original TNG-DM halo. We retain only halo pairs for which $>70\%$ of particles successfully return to the original halo in this bidirectional matching, ensuring robust correspondence. This procedure effectively filters out halos that exist exclusively in one simulation or the other due to differences in baryonic physics affecting structure formation. 

For all subsequent references to halos in this manuscript, we refer to this catalog of bijectively matched halos rather than the full FoF/SUBFIND catalogs. At $z=0$, this matched catalog contains approximately $1.2\times 10^6$ halos with $M_{200} > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$. We define $R_{200}$ as the radius enclosing a mean density 200 times the critical density $\rho_{\rm crit}(z)$ of the universe at that redshift, and $M_{200}$ as the total mass (dark matter plus baryons) enclosed within that radius. To avoid ambiguity where halo regions overlap, we adopt a mass-based priority rule: when multiple halos' replacement regions contain the same spatial location, only the most massive halo performs the replacement at that point. This ensures each particle position in the simulation volume is replaced at most once, preventing double-counting of baryonic effects.

\subsection{Replace Field Construction}

With the formalism outlined in \S~\ref{sec:formalism} and the matched halo catalogs described above, we construct a suite of Replace density fields by selectively replacing TNG-DM halo particles with their TNG counterparts. We partition the halo mass function into bins with edges $M_i = [10^{12}, 10^{12.5}, 10^{13}, 10^{13.5}, 10^{15})\,h^{-1}\,M_\odot$, and radius space into shells with factors $\alpha_i = [0, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0, 5.0]R_{200}$. This yields a 4$\times$4 grid in mass-radius space, plus cumulative configurations, for a total of approximately 100 distinct Replace field variants.

We distinguish between \emph{exclusive} models, which replace halos only within a single mass-radius tile (e.g., $10^{13} < M < 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ and $0.5 < \alpha < 1.0$), and \emph{cumulative} models, which include all halos above a mass threshold and within a cumulative radius factor (e.g., $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ and $\alpha < 3.0$). Mixed configurations—cumulative in mass but exclusive in radius, or vice versa—allow us to isolate the contributions from specific radial shells across the full mass range, or from specific mass bins across all radii.

In Figure~\ref{fig:models}, we show a grid representing each Replace field model. The upper left corner represents exclusive models, while the lower right corner shows purely exclusive models. The offdiagonal elements show combinations of cumulative mass/radius bins with exclusive radius/mass bin combinations. The color of each tile indicates the volume filling fraction of the replacement region within the TNG300-1 box at $z=0$. This was simply computed with the radii from the halo catalogs to compute and sum spherical volumes, with respect to the total box volume. If anything, this volume filling fraction is a slight over estimate as it does not account for overlaps between halos, but as the halos make up a minority of the volume, this correction is likely small. We only use the volume filling fraction so the reader can get a rough sense of the amount of replacement that takes place, and therefore a small overestimate of the volume filling fraction is acceptable. The most cumulative configuration, replacing halos with $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$, modifies approximately 5\% of the total simulation volume. Lower-mass halos and smaller radii naturally occupy smaller volume fractions, with the exclusive bin models each affecting $\lesssim 0.1\%$ of the volume.

\subsection{Ray-Tracing Pipeline}\label{sec:raytracing}

We employ a multi-lens-plane ray-tracing approach to generate weak lensing convergence and shear maps from the Replace density fields, following the methodology of \citet{}. We briefly summarize the key steps here and refer the reader to these works for full implementation details.

Light rays are traced backwards from an observer at $z=0$ to a source redshift $z_s$. To construct light cones spanning comoving distances up to $\sim 4000\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$ (corresponding to $z_s \approx 2.5$), we stack TNG300-1 snapshots along the line of sight at fixed intervals equal to the box size, $205\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$. This requires 20 snapshots in total. Following \citet{}, we select the TNG snapshots closest to the centres of these 20 equally-spaced comoving distance intervals. The specific snapshot numbers, redshifts, and comoving distances are listed in Table~1 of \citet{}.

Each snapshot is then split into two planes spaced at regular intervals of half the TNG300-1 comoving box size, $\Delta \chi = 102.5\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$. At each plane, we project the 3D density field $\rho(\bm{x},z)$ onto a 2D surface density $\Sigma(\bm{\theta},\chi)$ by integrating along the line of sight over the plane thickness:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_plane}
\Sigma(\bm{\theta}, \chi_i) = \int_{\chi_i - \Delta\chi/2}^{\chi_i + \Delta\chi/2} \rho(\chi \bm{\theta}, \chi)\, d\chi,
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\theta}$ is the angular position on the sky and $\chi$ is comoving distance. Following $\kappa$TNG, we perform ray tracing in a $5\times 5\deg^2$ light cone. The last 10 snapshots ($z \gtrsim 0.8$) are replicated 4 times in the transverse direction to achieve the desired angular coverage at high redshift \citep{}.

The deflection angle $\bm{\alpha}$ accumulated by a light ray passing through lens plane $i$ is computed from the 2D gravitational potential via
\begin{equation}
\bm{\alpha}_i(\bm{\theta}) = \frac{2}{c^2} \int \nabla_{\perp} \Phi(\bm{\theta}', \chi_i) \frac{d^2\theta'}{|\bm{\theta} - \bm{\theta}'|},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is related to $\Sigma$ through the 2D Poisson equation. In practice, we solve this in Fourier space using Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) on a $4096^2$ grid spanning the light cone opening angle of $5\times 5\deg^2$. Dark matter, gas, and stellar particles (or, for Replace fields, the hybrid density distribution) are assigned to the grid using a triangular-shaped cloud (TSC) scheme \citep{}.

The convergence $\kappa$ and shear $\gamma$ fields are derived from second derivatives of the lensing potential accumulated across all lens planes:
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\bm{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2} (\partial_1 \partial_1 + \partial_2 \partial_2)\psi(\bm{\theta}), \quad
\gamma_1(\bm{\theta)} = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_1\partial_1 - \partial_2\partial_2)\psi(\bm{\theta}), \quad
\gamma_2 = \partial_1\partial_2 \psi(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation}
where $\psi(\bm{\theta})$ is the effective lensing potential summed over planes weighted by the geometric lensing efficiency $g(\chi_s, \chi_i)$. The final convergence and shear maps are output on a $1024^2$ pixel grid with pixel size $0.29\,{\rm arcmin}$, matching the resolution used in the public $\kappa$TNG  suite.

To suppress sample variance, we generate multiple pseudo-independent realizations of each model-- Replace, hydro and dmo -- by exploiting the periodic boundary conditions and the fact that the light cone does not cover the entire simulation box. For each model, we randomly translate all particles by a random offset along each of the three axes, rotate the snapshot by 0, 90, 180, or 270 degrees around each axis, and apply a random flip along any axis. This procedure is applied 20 times to each simulation snapshot, generating 20 unique sets of density planes and lensplanes. The ray tracing procedure then translates and rotates a set of lensplanes randomly, again allowing for the generation of 100 convergence maps per lensplane set. All together, we end up with 2,000 convergence maps for each of the 100 Replace models as well as for TNG and TNG-DM. It is important to note that these convergence maps are pseudo-independant, meaning that they are random realizations of convergence maps, but were constructed from a simulation with the same initial conditions.

\section{The Baryonic Response of the Matter Field}
\label{sec:results_matter}

We begin our analysis with the matter power spectrum. Our approach replicates the halo replacement analysis of \citet{} and extends it across a systematic grid of mass bins, radial shells, and redshifts. We describe all results within the response formalism developed in \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, which provides a unified description of how different regions of halo parameter space contribute to baryonic effects. We first present cumulative models—replacing all halos above a mass threshold out to a fixed radius—to build intuition for the scale-dependent response. We then turn to exclusive models, which isolate individual mass-radius tiles, and quantify the degree of non-additivity following Eq.~\eqref{eq:non_additivity}.

For each Replace model shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:models}, we apply the replacement procedure of Eq.~\eqref{eq:replace_bin} to the 20 TNG300-1 snapshots spanning redshifts $0.03 \leq z \leq 2.44$ (Table~1 of \citealt{}). At each snapshot, after splitting the box in half, particles are assigned to two $4096^2$ pixel grids using triangle-shaped cloud (TSC) interpolation one representing the front half of the box, and the other representing the rear half of the box. We compute the overdensity field for each model $i$ and redshift $z$ as
%
\begin{equation}
\delta_i(\bm{x}, z) = \frac{\rho_i(\bm{x}, z) - \bar{\rho}_i(z)}{\bar{\rho}_i(z)},
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar{\rho}_i(z)$ is the mean density of model $i$ at redshift $z$. The matter power spectrum is then computed via
%
\begin{equation}
P_i(k, z) = \langle |\tilde{\delta}_i(\bm{k}, z)|^2 \rangle,
\end{equation}
%
where $\tilde{\delta}_i$ is the Fourier transform of $\delta_i$ and angle brackets denote averaging over Fourier modes.

We now apply the response formalism of \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, replacing the generic statistic subscript $S$ with $P$ for the "power spectrum". We begin with cumulative models represented by the $16$ tiles in the lower right corner of Fig.~\ref{fig:models}, and for which all halos with mass $M > M_{\min}$ are replaced within $r < \alpha R_{200}$. To avoid numerical instabilities when the denominator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cum_resp_func} approaches zero, we compute response fractions $F_P(M_{\min}, \alpha; k, z)$ only at $k$ where baryonic suppression exceeds 1\%: $|P_{\rm H}(k,z) - P_{\rm D}(k,z)|/P_{\rm D}(k,z) > 0.01$. Additionally, all power spectra are evaluated up to the Nyquist frequency of the density grid, $k_{\rm max} = \pi N / L = 62.7\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ with $N=4096$ pixels.

Figure~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} shows the power spectrum suppression and cumulative response fractions for the front half of the $z=0.02$ snapshot for the 16 cumulative models spanning our mass-radius grid. In the top panel, the black curve shows the full hydrodynamical suppression $\langle P_{\rm H}(k)\rangle/\langle P_{\rm D}(k)\rangle$, where the mean is computed over the 20 pseudo-independant realizations. Each column shows the ratio  $P_{\rm R}(k)/P_{\rm D}(k)$ for Replace models with varying $M_{\min}$ and colors represent the different radius factors $\alpha$. The bottom panel shows the response fractions in the same format, with panels representing $M_{\rm min}$and colors showing the different $\alpha$ factors. 

There are various interesting aspects of this plot. First, even the most aggressive replacement — all halos with $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$ captures only $\sim 90\%$ of the total hydrodynamical suppression at $1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}\lesssim k \lesssim 50 \,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, consistent with the findings of \citet{}. This shortfall implies that a non-negligible fraction of the baryonic signal originates from halos below our mass resolution limit, radii beyond $5R_{200}$, or the intergalactic medium not associated with halos. Second, models with small radius factors ($\alpha = 0.5$ or $1$) systematically \emph{enhance} power on large scales ($k < 1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$) relative to DMO, causing $P_{\rm R}/P_{\rm D} > 1$. This counterintuitive behavior arises from mass conservation violations and has been explored in \cite{}: replacing only halo cores with their lower-mass hydrodynamical counterparts removes mass from high-density peaks without compensating in the outskirts, artificially boosting large-scale power. This artifact disappears when $\alpha \geq 3$, indicating that the masss conservation between hydrodynamical and dark matter only simulations exists outside of the virial radius of halos. This has also been studied extensively in \cite{}

The bottom panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} show the cumulative response fraction $F_P(M_{\min}, \alpha; k)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:cum_resp_func},, where each column again represents mass bins, and the colors show the various $\alpha$ factors. By construction, $F_P = 0$ indicates the Replace model is indistinguishable from dark matter only, while $F_P = 1$ implies it fully reproduces the Hydrodynamical suppression. Values outside $[0,1]$ indicate artifacts of violating mass conservation, clearly visible in the $\alpha=0.5$ and $\alpha=1$ panels, where $F_P < 0$. 

The panels also qualitatively reveal the relative importance of mass bins. Galaxy clusters with $M > 10^{13}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ replaced out to $5R_{200}$ alone account for $F_P \sim 0.75$—seventy five percent of the total baryonic suppression—across $1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1} \lesssim k \lesssim 10\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. Extending the mass threshold from $10^{12.5}$ to $10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ yields an improvement at smaller scales enhancing $F_P$ to $\sim0.9$ at nearly all applicable scales.

To distill the scale-dependent information in Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} into a single metric per model, we compute the $k$-averaged fractional response $\langle F_P(M_{\min}, \alpha; z) \rangle_k $
where the sum extends over all $k$-bins between $k_{\min}(z)$—the minimum wavenumber at which the Hydro-DMO difference exceeds 1\% at redshift $z$—and the Nyquist frequency $k_{\rm max} = 62.7\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. Figure~\ref{fig:cum_response} presents $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ at $z=0$. The left panel shows $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ as a function of $M_{\min}$ for fixed $\alpha$. The right panel shows the complementary view: $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ as a function of $\alpha$ for fixed $M_{\min}$. 

The bottom panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:cum_response} displays $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ as a heat map over the full $(M_{\min}, \alpha)$ parameter space. This visualization provides a quick reference for model requirements. To achieve $\sim 10\%$ fidelity ($\langle F_P \rangle_k \gtrsim 0.9$, dark red), one must correctly reconstruct the density distribution of \emph{all} halos above $10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ out to at least $5R_{200}$. More modest goals, such as capturing 60\% of the effect ($\langle F_P \rangle_k \sim 0.6$,), can be met by focusing on massive halos ($M > 10^{13}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$) within $\sim 3R_{200}$. This quantitative guidance directly informs the calibration and validation strategies for baryonic feedback prescriptions in cosmological analyses.

We begin with the Gaussian statistics of the weak lensing field: the convergence angular power spectrum. For each convergence map, we compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C_l}
    C_{\kappa}(\ell_i) = \langle|\tilde{\kappa}(\bm{\ell})|^2\rangle_{\bm{\ell} \in [\ell_i^{\rm min}, \ell_i^{\rm max}]},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\kappa}(\bm{\ell})$ is the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the convergence field $\kappa(\bm{\theta})$, and the angle brackets denote averaging over all Fourier modes $\bm{\ell}$ within the multipole bin $[\ell_i^{\rm min}, \ell_i^{\rm max}]$. This yields angular power spectra for each of the 2,000 pseudo-independent maps across all Replace models and source redshifts. We compute the same quantities for the non-Replace models (i.e., pure TNG-Hydro and TNG-DMO), allowing us to define the cumulative response fraction for the convergence power spectrum following Eq.~\ref{eq:cum_resp_func}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_cl}
    F_{C_\ell}(M_{\min}, \alpha; \ell, z_s) = \frac{\langle C_\ell^{\rm R}(M_{\min}, \alpha; z_s) \rangle - \langle C_\ell^{\rm DMO}(z_s) \rangle}{\langle C_\ell^{\rm H}(z_s) \rangle - \langle C_\ell^{\rm DMO}(z_s) \rangle},
\end{equation}
where the outer angle brackets denote averaging over the 2,000 map realizations. As in \S~\ref{sec:results_matter}, we begin by presenting results for the 16 cumulative models in the lower-right corner of Fig.~\ref{fig:models}, followed by additivity and non-linearity from exclusive models.

The black curve in the top panel shows the full hydrodynamical suppression $C_\ell^{\rm H}/C_\ell^{\rm DMO}$.

This indicates that baryonic modifications to the convergence power spectrum at $z_s \sim 1$ are dominated by processes occurring within identified halos above our mass threshold, with minimal contribution from the IGM or low-mass halos. 

Models with small radius factors ($\alpha = 0.5$ or $1$, dashed and dotted lines) show suppression at large scales ($\ell \lesssim 500$), causing $C_\ell^{\rm R}/C_\ell^{\rm DMO} < 1$ and pushing $F_{C_\ell}$ above 1. This is a key difference from the matter power spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:cum_response}), where extending to $5R_{200}$ provided more significant gains.

The bottom heat map reveals that replacing halos with $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ out to just $R_{200}$ (i.e., $\alpha = 1$) already captures $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell \sim 0.85$—approximately 85\% of the baryonic effect on the convergence power spectrum at $z_s \sim 1$. To achieve $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell \gtrsim 0.9$, one must extend to at least $3R_{200}$ for halos above $10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$. This is a less stringent requirement than for the matter power spectrum, which required $5R_{200}$ to approach 90\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:cum_response}). The difference arises because the lensing kernel integrates the density field along the line of sight and across a range of redshifts, effectively smoothing over some of the small-scale radial structure that dominates the matter power spectrum at high $k$.

Finally, figure~\ref{fig:C_ell_redshift_evolution} extends this analysis across source redshifts from $z_s = 0.03$ to $z_s = 2.57$. The layout mirrors Fig.~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution}, with each panel showing $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell$ as a function of source redshift for fixed $\alpha$ and varying $M_{\min}$.

The convergence power spectrum response shows much weaker redshift dependence than what we saw in the matter power spectrum. For models with $\alpha \geq 3$, the response remains relatively flat at $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell$. Again, we believe this reflects the fact that lensing integrates contributions along the line of sight, averaging over the redshift-dependent baryonic effects that were starkly evident in the snapshot-by-snapshot matter power spectra (Fig.~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution}). 

Unlike the matter power spectrum, the convergence power spectrum response does not show a clear signature of low-mass halos "turning off" their feedback at high redshift. This is again due to projection effects: even though individual low-mass halos at $z \sim 2$ may exhibit weak feedback (as seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution}), the lensing signal for high-$z_s$ sources is dominated by more evolved halos at intermediate lens redshifts where feedback is active.

These results demonstrate that accurately modeling baryonic effects on the weak lensing convergence power spectrum for Stage-III and Stage-IV surveys requires capturing halos down to at least $M \sim 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$—group-scale systems that dominate the lensing-weighted baryonic signal and extending halo modifications to at least $3R_{200}$, and preferably $5R_{200}$ for percent-level accuracy at high multipoles. These quantitative constraints provide direct guidance for calibrating baryonic correction models (BCMs) and for interpreting cosmological parameter constraints from upcoming weak lensing surveys.

\subsection{Beyond Gaussian statistics}

Replacing halo cores within $0.5R_{200}$ (leftmost panels) captures $F_{\rm peak} \sim 0.6$--$0.8$ for high-$\nu$ peaks ($\nu \gtrsim 4$), demonstrating that baryonic effects on peak counts are dominated by the innermost halo regions. By $\alpha = 3$ (third column), the response saturates at $F_{\rm peak} \gtrsim 0.95$ across all $\nu$, indicating that peak statistics are mostly insensitive to baryonic effects beyond $\sim 3R_{200}$.}
    \label{fig:peaks_by_radius}

We now extend our response analysis to non-Gaussian statistics of the weak lensing convergence field, which probe the matter distribution in qualitatively different ways than the power spectrum. We focus on four complementary statistics computed from the convergence maps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Peak counts} $N(\nu)$: The number of local maxima in the convergence field per unit area as a function of signal-to-noise ratio $\nu = \kappa/\sigma_\kappa$, where $\sigma_\kappa = \sqrt{\langle \kappa^2 \rangle}$ is the root-mean-square convergence \citep{}.
\item \textbf{Minimum counts} $N_{\rm min}(\nu)$: The number of local minima in the convergence field, sensitive to underdense regions and their interplay with baryonic feedback.
\item \textbf{One-point PDF} $p(\kappa)$: The probability distribution of convergence values, capturing the full shape of the convergence histogram.
\item \textbf{Minkowski functionals} (MFs): Three morphological descriptors of convergence excursion sets \citep{}—area $V_0(\nu)$, boundary length $V_1(\nu)$, and genus $V_2(\nu)$—that encode shape information.
\end{enumerate}

These statistics complement the angular power spectrum by accessing non-Gaussian features of the matter distribution which have been shown to hold a wealth of cosmological information \cite{}. For brevity, we present detailed results for peak counts in the main text; analogous analyses for minima, the PDF, and Minkowski functionals are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_nongaussian}.

For each of the $\sim 8$ million convergence maps (100 Replace models $\times$ 40 source redshifts $\times$ 2,000 realizations), we compute these statistics on the unsmoothed, noise-free convergence field. Peak and minimum counts are identified as pixels with convergence values greater/less than all eight directly neighboring pixels \citep{}. We bin these counts in 50 linearly spaced $\nu$ bins spanning $-5 \leq \nu \leq 10$. The cumulative and exclusive response fractions are defined analogously to the power spectrum case:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:F_peak}
F_{\rm peak}(M_{\min}, \alpha; \nu, z_s) = \frac{\langle N_{\rm R}(\nu; M_{\min}, \alpha, z_s) \rangle - \langle N_{\rm D}(\nu; z_s) \rangle}{\langle N_{\rm H}(\nu; z_s) \rangle - \langle N_{\rm D}(\nu; z_s) \rangle},
\end{equation}

where $N_{\rm R}$, $N_{\rm D}$, and $N_{\rm H}$ are the peak counts in Replace, DMO, and Hydro maps, respectively, and angle brackets denote averaging over the 2,000 map realizations. Uncertainties are propagated from the map ensemble variance via
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{F_{\rm peak}}^2 = \frac{1}{(N_{\rm H} - N_{\rm D})^2} \left[ \sigma_{N_{\rm R}}^2 + F_{\rm peak}^2 \left( \sigma_{N_{\rm H}}^2 + \sigma_{N_{\rm D}}^2 \right) \right],
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{N_i}$ is the standard deviation of peak counts across realizations for model $i$.

Figure~\ref{fig:peaks_by_radius} shows the cumulative response of peak counts to baryonic effects as a function of $\nu$ at source redshift $z_s \approx 1.0$. The top panels display the ratio $N_{\rm R}/N_{\rm D}$ for different $M_{\min}$ at fixed $\alpha$, while the bottom panels show the response fraction $F_{\rm peak}$. Several striking features emerge that differ qualitatively from the convergence power spectrum results (Fig.~\ref{fig:C_ell_scale_dependent_response_z1}):

First, peak counts are dominated by halo core modifications. The leftmost panel ($\alpha = 0.5$) shows that replacing only the innermost $0.5R_{200}$ of halos achieves $F_{\rm peak} \sim 0.6$--$0.8$ for high-$\nu$ peaks ($\nu \gtrsim 4$), capturing 60--80\% of the total baryonic effect. High-$\nu$ convergence peaks are sourced by individual massive halos whose lensing signal is determined primarily by the projected density within $\sim R_{200}$ \citep{} and this baryonic processes that contract or expand the core density profile—such as AGN feedback evacuating gas from cluster centers or stellar feedback redistributing baryons in groups—directly modulate peak heights.

Second, peak statistics saturate at smaller radii than the power spectrum. By $\alpha = 3$ (third panel), $F_{\rm peak} \gtrsim 0.95$ across nearly all $\nu$, indicating that baryonic modifications beyond $3R_{200}$ contribute negligibly to peak counts. Extending to $\alpha = 5$ (fourth panel) yields marginal improvement. This contrasts with the power spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:cum_response}), which required $\alpha = 5$ to approach 90\% fidelity. The difference arises because peak counts probe the local density contrast around individual halos, whereas the power spectrum integrates over all Fourier modes including those sensitive to large-scale correlations between halos and their extended outskirts.

% Third, the $\nu$-dependence of the response is non-monotonic for intermediate $\alpha$. At $\alpha = 1$ (second panel), the response fraction dips slightly for $\nu \sim 3$--$5$ before rising again at higher $\nu$. This reflects competing effects: at moderate $\nu$, peaks arise from projections of multiple intermediate-mass halos, whose collective contribution is less sensitive to individual halo cores; at high $\nu \gtrsim 6$, peaks are dominated by single massive clusters whose core modifications matter more. This is consistent with analytic modeling showing that intermediate-$\nu$ peaks ($\nu \sim 3$--$5$) are the most sensitive to large-scale structure projection effects and least sensitive to individual halo profiles \citep{}.

The left panel, showing the innermost core region $0 \leq r < 0.5R_{200}$, exhibits clear mass structure in $\nu$-space: low-mass halos ($10^{12}$--$10^{13}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$, purple/blue curves) dominate the response at $\nu \sim 2.5$--$4$, groups ($10^{13}$--$10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$, yellow) control $\nu \sim 5$--$8$, and clusters ($M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$, red) dominate the highest peaks $\nu \gtrsim 8$. The middle and right panels reveal a qualitatively different behavior for halo outskirts ($r > 0.5R_{200}$): the exclusive response $\Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin}$ is approximately constant in $\nu$ at $\sim 0.05$ for all radial shells beyond $R_{200}$. The largest effect comes from cluster outskirts ($M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$, red curves in second from the right panel), where AGN-driven gas redistribution at $1R_{200} < r < 3R_{200}$ contributes $\Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \sim 0.1$ uniformly, consistent with recent findings that AGN feedback expels significant mass to $\sim 2$--$3R_{200}$ in massive systems \citep{}.

The left panel ($0 \leq r < 0.5R_{200}$) shows that at low source redshifts ($z_s \lesssim 0.5$), low-mass halos dominate the peak response, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu \sim 0.4$ for $10^{12.5}$--$10^{13}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ (purple and light blue curves). Conversely, at high source redshifts ($z_s \gtrsim 1.5$), only more massive halos contribute, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu \sim 0.2$ for $M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ (red curve) and $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu \sim 0.35$ for the intermediate and more massive groups $10^{12.5}$--$10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ (blue and yellow curves).

The middle and right panels show that outer radial shells ($r > 0.5R_{200}$) exhibit much weaker redshift evolution, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu$ remaining roughly constant at $\sim 0.05$--$0.15$ across all $z_s$. This insensitivity to source redshift indicates that gas at $r \sim R_{200}$--$5R_{200}$ contributes a smooth convergence signal that boosts the peak counts uniformly.

These results have direct implications for cosmological analyses using weak lensing peak counts. For all peak count analyses capturing the cores of halos is of primary importance. But further, one can use this informaiton to cross validate BCMs. The distinct $\nu$-$M$ structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:peaks_discrete_by_radius}, left panel) means that BCMs can be tested by checking whether they reproduce the correct peak counts at different $\nu$ thresholds. A BCM that fits high-$\nu$ peaks ($\nu \gtrsim 8$, cluster-dominated) but fails at $\nu \sim 3$--$5$ (group-dominated) reveals inconsistent treatment of feedback across mass scales.

In Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_nongaussian}, we present response analyses analogous to Figs.~\ref{fig:peaks_by_radius}, \ref{fig:peaks_discrete_by_radius}, and \ref{fig:peaks_redshift_evolution} for minimum counts, the one-point PDF, Minkowski functionals, and wavelet scattering coefficients. Here, we synthesize these results through two comparative heatmaps representing the main findings of this section.

Figure~\ref{fig:all_statistics_cumulative_heatmap} compares the cumulative mean response $\langle F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) \rangle$ across five weak lensing statistics at source redshift $z_s \approx 1.0$. Each panel represents a different statistic, and each cell shows the response for a given cumulative $(M_{\min}, \alpha)$ configuration, with color intensity representing the level of baryonic effect captured. 

We see that even the most aggressive halo replacement fails to fully reproduce baryonic effects for most statistics. The bottom-right cells of each heatmap—corresponding to replacing all halos with $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$—achieve $\langle F_S \rangle \sim 0.95$--$1.0$ only for peak counts, the area Minkowski functional $V_0$, and the PDF. The second Minkowski functional, $V_1$ has the largest gap, with $\langle F_{V_1} \rangle \sim 0.88$.

Statistics that exhibit this failure to reach $F_S = 1$ even with perfect field level replacements suggests physical mechanisms beyond our modeling framework: (1) baryonic effects in halos below our mass threshold ($M < 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$). These mass ranges were neglected from this work primarily because the low mass halos are not included in standard baryon correction models. This is typically the case, because the physically motivated baryonic correction model parameters are ones which can be fit to X-ray and SZ observations, limiting them to higher mass halos. (2) The diffuse intergalactic medium (IGM)such as gas in filaments or galactic outflows extending far beyond the virial radii of halos. And (3) The non-additivity and inter-halo coupling, where mass redistribution between halo cores and outskirts cannot be seperated into independent contributions. The fact that peak counts do reach $F_{\rm peak} \approx 1$ suggests they are uniquely insensitive to these diffuse components, consistent with their status as local, halo-centric statistics.

Fig.~\ref{fig:all_statistics_cumulative_heatmap} also represents an important point for BCM validation. NAmely, any baryon model that fits a given statistic presented here to sub-percent precision must, by construction, violate field-level accuracy. For example, a BCM calibrated to match the observed convergence power spectrum to $\Delta C_\ell / C_\ell < 1\%$ (a typical target for Stage-IV surveys and fit claims in BCM papers) cannot simultaneously place field level baryonic effects in the correct locations within/outside of halos, because even our Replace procedure (which uses the true Hydro field level distribution) falls short by $\sim 8\%$. This means that BCMs should be interpreted as models that reproduce summary statistics as opposed to a true physical representation of baryonic effects. This distinction becomes critical when combining multiple observables: a BCM tuned to $C_\ell$ will predict incorrect peak counts, void counts, or higher-order correlations if those statistics weight different regions of halo parameter space.

It is also clear that different statistics exhibit different saturation scales in the $M-R$ space. Peak counts achieve $\langle F_{\rm peak} =1$ by $\alpha = 3$ for $M_{\min} = 10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$, whereas the convergence power spectrum requires extension to $\alpha = 5$ to reach $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle \sim 0.93$. Minimum counts fall between these extremes, saturating around $\alpha = 3$--$5$ with $\langle F_{\rm min} \rangle \sim 0.88$ for $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$. The PDF and Minkowski functionals show the most gradual saturation, with visible color gradients persisting across all $\alpha$ values. 

Figure~\ref{fig:all_statistics_discrete_heatmap} breaks these cumulative responses into exclusive mass-radius tiles, showing the detailed spatial origin of each statistic's sensitivity. We again see that peak counts are uniquely core-dominated. The single tile corresponding to group-scale halo cores. Minimum counts show enhanced sensitivity to low-mass halo cores compared to peaks. The minimum count heatmap displays brighter colors in the $10^{12}$--$10^{12.5}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ row at $r < 0.5R_{200}$ than the corresponding cells in the peak heatmap, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm min}^{\rm bin} \rangle \sim 0.16$ versus $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle \sim 0.07$. This inversion implies that baryonic effects in weak lensing minima, which we know to be sourced by voids and low-density regions, are strongly influenced by low-mass halos most likely through stellar feedback. Peaks, being sourced by overdensities, are less sensitive to this process. This differential response suggests that combining peak and minimum counts can potentially break degeneracies in BCM parameters. For example a model that matches peaks by suppressing cluster cores but matches minima by enhancing low-mass outflows is more physically constrained than one tuned to either statistic alone. We also see that Minkowski functionals exhibit the largest sensitivity to the massive galaxy clusters. The heatmaps shows.

Finally, we note that these results are derived at a single source redshift $z_s \approx 1.0$. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_nongaussian} extends the analysis across $z_s \in [0.03, 2.57]$ for all statistics, revealing that the qualitative patterns in Figs.~\ref{fig:all_statistics_cumulative_heatmap}--\ref{fig:all_statistics_discrete_heatmap} persist across redshift, though the relative importance of different mass bins shifts (as shown for peaks in Fig.~\ref{fig:peaks_redshift_evolution}). For tomographic weak lensing analyses that bin sources by redshift, this implies that BCM requirements evolve with source bin: low-$z_s$ bins demand accurate modeling of low-mass halos, whereas high-$z_s$ bins can tolerate larger uncertainties at $M \lesssim 10^{13}\,h^{-1}M_\odot$ as long as cluster cores are well-calibrated.
