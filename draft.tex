\begin{abstract}
\noindent Upcoming weak lensing surveys like Euclid, LSST, and Roman require percent-level control over systematic effects, such as those induced by baryons. However, existing halo-based baryonic correction schemes have not been convincingly validated across multiple statistics, which may be sensitive to baryonic physics effects in different ways. We introduce a response framework quantifying the origin of baryonic effects, in halo mass-halocentric radius space, on cosmological statistics such as the matter and angular power spectra, weak lensing peak counts, minima, one-point PDFs, and Minkowski functionals. To do so, we create hybrid ``Replace" density fields by selectively injecting hydrodynamical particles from TNG300 into its twin dark-matter-only simulation across a grid of halo mass bins and radial shells. We find that incomplete field-level modeling imposes fundamental limits on halo-based correction schemes. Even replacing all haloes with $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $5R{200}$ recovers only $\sim 90\%$ of baryonic suppression in $P(k)$ and $C_\ell$; the remaining $\sim 10\%$ originates from lower-mass haloes or diffuse intergalactic gas. Each statistic exhibits a distinct "baryonic fingerprint": while $P(k)$ and $C_\ell$ respond broadly across mass and radius, weak lensing peaks and the one-point PDF localize to cores with $M\gtrsim 10^{12}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ and $r<R{200}$. Existing baryonic correction models calibrated to massive halos and matter power spectrum under-predict core masses by $\sim 20\%$ for $M<10^{13}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$, naturally explaining previously reported failures for peak statistics. Our framework provides a detailed diagnostic for critical regions in baryonic modeling and a roadmap for next-generation baryonification schemes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Realizing the full scientific potential of upcoming weak gravitational lensing surveys such as Euclid \citep{Euclid11}, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) \citep{LSST}, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope \citep{Roman15} requires percent-level control of systematic uncertainties, including the impact of baryonic physics on the matter power spectrum as well as higher-order statistics \citep{Chisari19}. This is complicated by baryonic feedback, which redistributes matter within and around dark matter halos \citep{schaye10, McCarthy17, Volker10, Nelson2019, hadzhiyska_evidence_2024, bigwood2024weaklensingcombinedkinetic} and has been shown to affect statistics such as suppressing the matter power spectrum by 10–20\% at scales $k \sim 10\,h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$ \citep{Arico2020, Osato21} and altering non-Gaussian statistics, such as weak lensing peak counts, minima, and Minkowski functionals \citep{jain00, Liu15, Martinet2021, leeComparingWeakLensing2022}. If left untreated, these effects can bias cosmological parameter constraints by amounts exceeding the statistical uncertainties of Stage-IV data \citep{Hikage19, Hamana20, Amon-2020, DES21, Abbott22, Abbot-26}.

Hydrodynamical simulations self-consistently model gas physics, star formation, and feedback via parameterized subgrid models at a resolution below that of the simulations. These implementations have been shown to accurately predict baryonic effects, but hydrodynamical simulations remain computationally prohibitive for the large volumes required by modern surveys \citep{schaye10, Pillepich17, Nelson2019}. A single flagship hydrodynamical simulation run, such as IllustrisTNG or FLAMINGO, requires tens of millions of CPU hours \citep{Nelson2019, Flamingo-23} and typically provides a single simulation realization at one or a few locations in a high-dimensional subgrid model parameter space\footnote{Subgrid models such as the ones from IllustrisTNG can have upwards of 28 parameters \citep{Ni2023}. The CAMELS suites of simulations have performed systematic explorations of this space along with other subgrid parameter models in \citep{camels20, Ni2023}, but are limited to smaller cosmological box sizes.}. These simulations cannot be easily repeated across the subgrid parameter space required to marginalize over these physical effects.

This computational expense has motivated the development of semi-analytic empirical \emph{baryonic correction models} (BCMs), which attempt to capture baryonic effects by modifying dark-matter-only (DMO) simulation particles with prescriptions for gas ejection, stellar fractions, and dark matter relaxation modifications \citep{Schneider-15, Schneider19, Arico2020, Arico20b, Anbajagane-24, Schneider-25}. BCMs can be calibrated to match statistics measured in hydrodynamical simulations \citep{Arico+2021,Zhou25}, or fit to external observables such as Sunyaev–Zel’dovich (SZ) and X-ray data \citep{Schneider19, Schneider-25, Grandis-24}, making them physically motivated alternatives to full hydrodynamical simulations.

Despite their computational efficiency, BCMs face a fundamental question of physical self-consistency: if a model is tuned to reproduce a statistic—for example, the 3D matter power spectrum $P(k)$—does it automatically reproduce the correct baryonic impact on other observables, such as the weak lensing convergence power spectrum $C_\ell$ \citep{heymans13, kitching14, hildebrandt17, Hikage19}, peak counts and minima \citep{jain00, yang11, Liu15, kacprzak16, Li2019, Martinet2021, Zurcher2021, Harnois2021}, one-point convergence probability distribution functions \citep{thiele_accurate_2020}, or Minkowski functinoals \citep{Maturi+2010,Munshi11, Kratochvil+2012, petri13, Marques2019, Gatti21}?

Recent comparisons of BCMs with hydrodynamical simulations have revealed discrepancies. A BCM may match $P(k)$ at the few-percent level while failing to reproduce peak count distributions \citep{leeComparingWeakLensing2022} or bispectra \citep{Arico+2021} at the same level of accuracy. More fundamentally, \citet{Miller-25} demonstrated that even a direct procedure of replacing halo particles in an N-body dark matter only simulation with particles from their matched hydrodynamical counterparts out to $2R_{200}$ in IllustrisTNG does not fully recover the hydrodynamical matter power spectrum. This discrepancy implies that some of the baryonic effect is sourced from lower-mass halos or diffuse intergalactic components \citep{Miller-25, Ayromlou-23}. Moreover, even if field-level Replacements in dark matter only simulations set to match the effects of hydrodynamics perfectly fail to reconstruct $P(k)$, then BCMs tuned to $P(k)$ must be compensating by placing baryonic modifications in regions of halo mass and radius space that are inconsistent with their hydrodynamical halo counterparts. If this is the case, these modifications may propagate inconsistently to other statistics.

This raises the question this work seeks to answer: Which halo masses and halocentric radii dominate the baryonic impact on a given statistic? Understanding this is essential for determining where in mass-radius space baryonic models should provide the most accurate predictions and where approximations are more tolerable. Further, answering this provides a method for testing whether a BCM is self-consistent across multiple statistics. Addressing this question requires a unified framework to quantify how arbitrary statistics respond to baryonic modifications across halo-mass radial-shell bins.

We introduce a self-consistent formalism that addresses this by defining a family of hybrid ``Replace" fields, in which N-body simulation particles within radial bins $r_i, r_j$ and halo masses $M_a, M_b$ at redshift $z$ are Replaced by particles from a hydrodynamical simulation. By applying this replacement over a set of models, we can build a map of where baryonic effects on different statistics originate. Importantly, we can apply the formalism to any summary statistic, such as matter power spectra, weak-lensing two-point functions, peak counts, minima, PDFs, and Minkowski functionals, enabling direct comparisons of response patterns across statistics.

We apply this framework to IllustrisTNG simulations, computing baryonic responses for the matter power spectrum, weak lensing angular power spectrum, and several non-Gaussian statistics. We then consider the placement of baryons by BCMs and their influence on measured statistics.

This paper is organized as follows. In \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, we develop the cumulative and discrete response formalisms and establish the mathematical properties of response fractions. In \S~\ref{sec:simulations}, we describe the IllustrisTNG DMO and hydrodynamical runs, the halo-matching procedure, the raytracing algorithm, and the construction of the Replacement density fields. \S~\ref{sec:results_matter} presents response kernels for matter power spectra, testing additivity and quantifying the contribution of different mass–radial shells. \S~\ref{sec:results_WL} extends the analysis to weak lensing observables, including convergence power, peak counts, minima, and Minkowski functionals. We then consider the full baryonic fingerprint of various statistics in \S~\ref{sec:fingerprint}, and in \S~\ref{sec:BCM} we discuss the implications for several popular baryon-correction models in the literature. We summarize our findings and discuss future extensions in \S~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{A Response Formalism}
\label{sec:formalism}
We begin with a general treatment of a statistic’s response to baryonic injections. We take as our inspiration the ``separate universe simulations" of \citet{Li14} \& \citet{Wagner15}, in which the authors studied the response of the power spectrum in the non-linear regime to the addition of a large-scale background mode. More recently, the separate-universe responses have been studied in \citet{Barreira19} using IllustrisTNG. Unlike the preceding work, we are interested in statistical responses to baryonic modifications within specific halo-mass and radius regions. To this end, we take a simplified, empirical approach: we treat the response of statistics to baryons as a function of mass and radius by simply modifying the field level of an N-body simulation to match that of a hydrodynamical simulation for the chosen mass and radius.

In general, we define a Replace mass field as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general_Replace_density}
m_{R}(\bm{x}, z) = m_D(\bm{x}, z)  + \sum_{i \in A} \left[m_H^i(\bm{x}, z) - m_{D}^i(\bm{x}, z)\right]
\end{equation}
where $m_D(\bm{x}, z)$ is the mass in a dark matter only simulation at coordinate $\bm{x}$ and redshift $z$. Given some set of coordinates described by $A$, the dark matter mass at that location, $m_D^i$, can be modified through a Replacement with the hydrodynamical mass, $m_H^i$, which includes any relevant dark matter, gas, stellar, or black hole particle. In the above, the framework is general such that $A$ can signify any chosen coordinates, but for this work, we will choose $A$ to be a set of halos with masses $M$ and radius factors $\alpha R_{200}$, where $\alpha$ represents some multiple of the radius, $R_{200}$ where the density is $200\times$ the cosmic density, $\rho_{c}$. To avoid double-counting when halo regions overlap, we assign priority in decreasing order of halo mass. For a given location $\bm{x}$, if multiple halos’ Replacement regions contain $\bm{x}$, only the most massive halo performs the Replacement at that location.
Mathematically, we define:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Replace}
m_R(\bm{x},z) = m_D(\bm{x},z) + \sum_{i \in A(M,z)} \theta_i(\bm{x})
\left[m_H^i(\bm{x},\alpha) - m_D^i(\bm{x},\alpha)\right],
\end{equation}

where the indicator function is
\begin{equation}
\theta_i(\bm{x}) = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } |\bm{x}-\bm{x}i| < \alpha R{200}^i \text{ and } i = \arg\max_j M_j
\text{ among all } j \text{ with } |\bm{x}-\bm{x}j| < \alpha R{200}^j, \
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This ensures that each spatial point receives Replacement from at most one halo.

In the limit of $M_{\rm min}\rightarrow 0$ or $\alpha\rightarrow\infty$, the field becomes purely hydro, and inversely, the field becomes purely DMO. It can then be thought of as $m_R$ interpolating between the DMO and hydrodynamical fields as the mass threshold and radius factor are varied.

For any statistic that we measure from the resulting Replace field ($S_R$), we can naively assume that its value is somewhere between the dark matter only ($S_D$) and hydrodynamical versions ($S_H$) of the same statistic, allowing us to define some response fraction,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cum_resp_func}
F_S(M_{\rm min}, \alpha) = \dfrac{S_R(M_{\rm min}, \alpha) - S_D}{S_H-S_D}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed $\alpha$ and redshift $z$, we can see that $F_s\rightarrow 0$ as $M_{\rm min}\rightarrow \infty$, and $F_s\rightarrow1$ as $M_{\rm min}\rightarrow0$ (so long as $\alpha$ is sufficiently large).

The fraction, $F_s$, is implicitly a function of the chosen statistics. For example, the matter power spectrum, $P(k)$, can be computed for hydro, DMO, and Replace fields, yielding a quantity, $F_P(M_{\rm min}, \alpha; k)$, which shows the masses and radius factors baryons contribute on a given scale. It is often the case that the hydrodynamical and DMO statistics are relatively consistent, for example, at large scales in the matter power spectrum. In such a case, the fraction becomes undefined, or can shoot above the 0-1 bound, so we must artificially fix the fraction to some constant, $F_p = c$, or remove such regions from our analysis altogether. In this work, we choose the latter, ignoring regions of $F_S$ where the hydro and DMO differ by $<1\%$.

It should also be noted that the fraction is not necessarily bounded between $0$ and $1$ if there truly exists significant dark matter only and hydrodynamical differences. The Replacement in this work does not necessarily follow mass conservation laws and can therefore create a statistic, $S_R$, that is no longer smoothly interpolated between the DMO and hydrodynamical statistics. For example, when cores of low-mass halos are Replaced with their hydrodynamical counterparts, they will have lower mass than their dark-matter-only counterparts due to feedback-driven expulsion. In such a case, we do not capture redistribution outside the halo cores, which may be important on some scales of a statistic. Our formalism does not address the origin of baryonic effects; it concerns the response of statistics to perfectly modelled effects in specific regions.

The above definitions using $M_{\rm min}$ are inherently cumulative as they incorporate all halos above some threshold mass, and Replacement regions in spheres according to $\alpha$. The response fraction then tells us, for a given statistic, how low and how far out we need to go to capture the full baryonic effects on the given statistic.

We can also define an analogous discrete version of Eq.~\ref{eq:cum_resp_func} where we partition halo masses and radii into finite bins and define the response from a specific mass-bin $[M_a, M_{a+1})$ and radial shell $[\alpha_i R_{200}, \alpha_{i+1} R_{200})$ combination. The ``tile" response fraction is then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:disc_resp_func}
\Delta F_S(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1}) =
\dfrac{S_R(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1}) - S_D}{S_H-S_D},
\end{equation}
where $S_R(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1})$ is the statistic measured from
The Replace field with halos in the specified mass-radius shell is activated.

If the responses to baryons in given mass and radial shells are linear, the cumulative and discrete responses are connected,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive_approx}
F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) \approx \sum_{\substack{a: M_a \geq M_{\min} \
i: \alpha_i \leq \alpha}}
\Delta F_S(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1}),
\end{equation}
where the sum extends over all mass-bin indices $a$ with $M_a \geq M_{\min}$
and all radius bin indices $i$ with $\alpha_i \leq \alpha$. Deviations from
This relation then quantifies the level of nonlinearity of the map $S[m]$. We can diagnose the level of this non-linearity at each scale of a statistic by defining the fractional non-additivity as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:non_additivity}
\epsilon_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) = \dfrac{F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) -
\sum_{\substack{a: M_a \geq M_{\min} \\ i: \alpha_i \leq \alpha}}
\Delta F_S(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i, \alpha_{i+1})}{F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha)}
\end{equation}
Values $|\epsilon_S| \approx 1$ indicate that mass-radius bins are separable to generate the baryonic effects, while deviations from $1$ indicate nonlinear
interactions or correlations between different regions of mass radius parameter space. A similar nonlinearity has been studied in \citep{Masaki20} using anisotropic separate-universe simulations, where the authors find that non-additive effects from large-scale tidal fields and two-halo terms lead to deviations from linearity. In our context, deviations likely reflect contributions from the two-halo term, arising either from replacements of halo exteriors, with neighboring halo fields likely affected as well, or from the statistic itself being non-linear.

Finally, we will often summarize a model's performance by taking the signal-to-noise (SNR) weighted mean of $F_S$. We do this by computing,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weighted_mean_response}
\langle F^M_S \rangle_x = \frac{\sum_x w(x) F^M_S(x)}{\sum_x w(x)}, \quad w(x) = \left(\frac{|S_{\rm H}(x) - S_{\rm D}(x)|}{S_{\rm D}(x)}\right)\left(\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{M,x}^2}\right),
\end{equation}
where the sum extends over all $x$-bins corresponding to a given statistic, $S$, (for example, $k$ for matter power spectrum, or $\ell$ for the angular power spectrum), the model $M$ corresponds to a model configuration in Replacement $M-\alpha$ space. The weights $w(k)$ are SNR-weights, where the ``signal" is the hydrodynamical to DMO difference, and the noise is from a given model’s measurement error. This weighting ensures that scales where baryonic effects are largest contribute most to the average, provided the model's measurements have low noise. In contrast, regions where the hydrodynamical and DMO models converge, or where noise levels are high, receive negligible weight. This yields a single number summarizing the overall effectiveness of a given $(M_{\min}, \alpha)$ or $(M_a, M_{a+1}; \alpha_i)$ configuration in capturing the baryonic signature on a given statistic.

\section{Simulations and Model Creation}
\label{sec:simulations}

\subsection{The IllustrisTNG Suite}

We use the highest-resolution version of the $205\,{\rm Mpc}\,h^{-1}$ box in the IllustrisTNG suite, specifically the TNG300-1 simulation, alongside its dark matter-only counterpart, IllustrisTNG-300-Dark~\citep[][hereafter TNG and TNG-Dark, respectively]{Pillepich17, springel18,Nelson2019,naiman18,Marinacci18} run with the adaptive moving-mesh code \texttt{AREPO} \citep{Volker10}. The TNG300-1 simulation spans a periodic volume with $205\,{\rm Mpc}\,h^{-1}$ on a side and evolves $2500^3$ dark matter and $2500^3$ gas particles, which can become stars and black holes, from redshift $z=127$ to $z=0$.

In practice, the formalism of \S~\ref{sec:formalism} can be applied to any simulation suite containing matched initial conditions between hydrodynamical and dark matter only counterparts. We choose IllustrisTNG for the present work to build upon and extend previous results examining baryonic effects through halo Replacement procedures \citep{Miller-25}, and our previous weak lensing statistic studies \citep{Osato21, leeComparingWeakLensing2022, Lee26}. Understanding the impact of different subgrid physics implementations on the response kernels derived in \S~\ref{sec:formalism} would be valuable, but we leave a systematic comparison across simulation suites (e.g., FLAMINGO \citep{Flamingo-23}, SIMBA \citep{SIMBA}, EAGLE \citep{EAGLE}) for future work.

Both TNG and TNG-DM adopt cosmological parameters consistent with Planck 2015 results: $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}=0.6911$, $\Omega_{m,0}=0.3089$, $\Omega_{b,0}=0.0486$, $\sigma_8=0.8159$, $n_s=0.9667$, and $h=0.6774$ \citep{Ade16}. The TNG simulation incorporates baryonic physics via a subgrid prescription including radiative cooling, star formation, stellar evolution and feedback, supermassive black hole seeding and growth, and AGN feedback \citep{springel18}. The model was calibrated to reproduce the galaxy stellar mass function, stellar-to-halo mass relation, and cosmic star formation rate density at $z\lesssim 10$ \citep{springel18}.

The mass resolution of TNG300-1 is $m_{\rm gas}=7.44\times 10^6\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ for initial gas cells and $m_{\rm DM}=3.98\times 10^7\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ for dark matter particles. The TNG-DM counterpart uses $m_{\rm DM}=4.72\times 10^7\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$, accounting for the cosmic baryon fraction. Both simulations output particle data at 100 snapshots spanning $0 \leq z \leq 20$, with all data publicly available through the IllustrisTNG data release \citep{Nelson2019}.

\subsection{Halo Catalogs and Matching}
Both TNG and TNG-DM employ the Friends-of-Friends (FoF) algorithm with linking length $b=0.2$ to identify dark matter halos, followed by the SUBFIND algorithm \citep{Springel01} to locate gravitationally bound substructures within each FoF group. These catalogs are computed on-the-fly during the simulation and output at each snapshot, with particle membership indexed to enable rapid halo-to-particle association. We define $R_{200}$ as the radius enclosing a mean density 200 times the critical density $\rho_{\rm crit}(z)$ of the universe at that redshift, and $M_{200}$ as the total mass (dark matter plus baryons) enclosed within that radius.

In \S~\ref{sec:BCM}, we compare density profiles from baryon correction models to hydrodynamical profiles from IllustrisTNG. To make such a comparison, we must choose a set of DMO halos to be baryonified and have a matching hydrodynamical halo counterpart. We accomplish this by constructing catalogs of bijectively matched halos between TNG-DM and TNG using a particle-based matching procedure. For each halo in TNG-DM, we identify all associated dark matter particles and determine their halo membership in TNG, selecting the TNG halo containing the largest fraction of these particles as the match. We then reverse the process: starting from the matched TNG halo, we verify that it maps back to the original TNG-DM halo. We retain only halo pairs for which $> 70\%$ of particles successfully return to their original halo in this bidirectional matching, guaranteeing robust correspondence \citep{Knebe11}. At $z=0$, this matched catalog contains approximately $1.2\times 10^6$ halos with $M_{200} > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$.

With the formalism outlined in \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, we construct a suite of Replace fields by selectively replacing TNG-DM halo particles with their TNG counterparts at various redshifts. We partition the halo mass function into bins with edges $M_i = [10^{12}, 10^{12.5}, 10^{13}, 10^{13.5}, \infty)\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ roughly corresponding to lower mass halos, Milky Way mass halos, galaxy groups, and galaxy clusters. We choose bins in radius space with shells of factors $\alpha_i = [0, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0, 5.0]R_{200}$ roughly corresponding to the core, one virial radius, the nearby outskirts, and the faraway outskirts. This yields a 4$\times$4 grid in mass-radius space, at each of the 20 snapshots from $z=0.03-2.56$ (chosen for the ray tracing procedure of \S~\ref{sec:raytracing}). Including combinations of both discrete in radius/mass and cumulative in radius/mass configurations, this yields 64 Replace field variants, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:models}.

We distinguish between \emph{discrete} models, which Replace halos only within a single mass-radius shell (e.g., $10^{13} < M < 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ and $0.5 < \alpha < 1.0$), and \emph{cumulative} models, which include all halos above a mass threshold and within a cumulative radius factor (e.g., $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ and $\alpha < 3.0$). Mixed configurations—cumulative in mass but discrete in radius, or vice versa—allow us to isolate the contributions from specific radial shells across the whole mass range, or from specific mass-bins across all radii. Note that the minimum possible Replacement, of $\alpha\in[0, 0.5] \cup \log_{10}(M/{\rm M}_{\odot})\in[13.5, \infty]$ is considered to be both discrete and cumulative according to our above definitions.

In Figure~\ref{fig:models}, we show a grid representing each Replace field model. The upper left corner represents discrete models, while the lower right corner shows purely discrete models (though we repeat the row for the minimum Replacement model that is both discrete and cumulative $\alpha\in[0, 0.5] \cup M\in[13.5, \infty]$ for clarity of plotting). The off-diagonal elements show combinations of cumulative mass/radius bins with discrete radius/mass-bin combinations. The color of each tile indicates the mass filling fraction of the Replacement region within the TNG300-1 box at $z=0$. This was computed summing over all particles in the DMO map remaining after a Replacement configuration was established. The most encompassing cumulative configuration, replacing halos with $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$, modifies approximately 50\% of the total simulation volume. Lower-mass halos and smaller radii naturally occupy smaller volume fractions, with the discrete bin models each affecting $\lesssim 0.1\%$ of the volume.

One consideration when performing the Replacement is the effect of rigid boundaries induced by a strict radial cutoff. In Appendix~A, we explore the possible effects of a hard Replacement vs. a softer weighted ramping. We find that the cutoff effects are less than 1\% across all relevant scales in this work and therefore retain the hard cutoff for clarity in interpreting our results.

\subsection{Ray-Tracing Pipeline}\label{sec:raytracing}

We employ a multi-lens-plane ray-tracing approach to generate weak lensing convergence from the Replace fields, following the methodology of \citet{jain2000, Hilbert09, Petri16a, Osato21, leeComparingWeakLensing2022, Ferlito24}. We briefly summarize the key steps here and refer the reader to these works for full implementation details.

Light rays are traced backwards from an observer at $z=0$ to a source redshift $z_s$. To construct light cones spanning comoving distances up to $\sim 4000\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$ (corresponding to $z_s \approx 2.56$), we stack Replace snapshots along the line of sight at fixed intervals equal to the box size, $205\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$. This requires 20 snapshots in total. Following \citet{Osato21}, we select the TNG snapshots closest to the centres of these 20 equally-spaced comoving distance intervals. The specific snapshot numbers, redshifts, and comoving distances are listed in Table~1 of \citet{Osato21}.

Each snapshot is then split into two planes spaced at regular intervals of half the TNG300-1 comoving box size, $\Delta \chi = 102.5\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}$. At each plane, we project the 3D mass field $\rho(\bm{x},z)$ onto a 2D surface density $\Sigma(\bm{\theta},\chi)$ by integrating along the line of sight over the plane thickness:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_plane}
\Sigma(\bm{\theta}, \chi_i) = \int_{\chi_i - \Delta\chi/2}^{\chi_i + \Delta\chi/2} \rho(\chi \bm{\theta}, \chi), d\chi,
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\theta}$ is the angular position on the sky and $\chi$ is comoving distance. Following $\kappa$TNG, we perform ray tracing in a $5\times 5\deg^2$ light cone. The last 10 snapshots ($z \gtrsim 0.8$) are replicated 4 times in the transverse direction to achieve the desired angular coverage at high redshift \citep{Osato21}.

The deflection angle $\bm{\alpha}$ accumulated by a light ray passing through lens plane $i$ is computed from the 2D gravitational potential via
\begin{equation}
\bm{\alpha}i(\bm{\theta}) = \frac{2}{c^2} \int \nabla{\perp} \Phi(\bm{\theta}‘, \chi_i) \frac{d^2\theta’}{|\bm{\theta} - \bm{\theta}'|},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is related to $\Sigma$ through the 2D Poisson equation. In practice, we solve this in Fourier space using Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) on a $4096^2$ grid spanning the light cone opening angle of $5\times 5\deg^2$. Dark matter, gas, and stellar particles (or, for Replace fields, the hybrid density distribution) are assigned to the grid using a triangular-shaped cloud (TSC) scheme.

The convergence $\kappa$ and shear $\gamma$ fields are derived from second derivatives of the lensing potential accumulated across all lens planes:
\begin{equation}
\kappa(\bm{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2} (\partial_1 \partial_1 + \partial_2 \partial_2)\psi(\bm{\theta}), \quad
\gamma_1(\bm{\theta)} = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_1\partial_1 - \partial_2\partial_2)\psi(\bm{\theta}), \quad
\gamma_2 = \partial_1\partial_2 \psi(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation}
where $\psi(\bm{\theta})$ is the effective lensing potential summed over planes weighted by the geometric lensing efficiency $g(\chi_s, \chi_i)$. The final convergence and shear maps are output on a $1024^2$ pixel grid with pixel size $0.29,{\rm arcmin}$, consistent with the resolution used in the public $\kappa$TNG  suite.

To suppress sample variance, we generate multiple pseudo-independent realizations of each Replace model by exploiting the periodic boundary conditions and the fact that the light cone does not cover the entire simulation box. For each model, we translate all particles by a random offset along each of the three axes, rotate the snapshot by 0, 90, 180, or 270 degrees around each axis, and apply a random flip along any axis. This procedure is applied 20 times to each simulation snapshot, yielding 20 distinct sets of density planes and lens planes. The ray tracing procedure then randomly translates and rotates a set of lens planes, thereby generating 100 convergence maps per lens-plane set. Altogether, we end up with 2,000 convergence maps for each of the 100 Replace models, as well as for TNG and TNG-DM. It is important to note that these convergence maps are pseudo-independent, meaning they are random realizations of convergence maps constructed from a simulation with the same initial conditions. As shown in \citet{Petri16}, with the above method, one can generate up to 10,000 statistically independent realizations.

\section{The Baryonic Response of the Matter Field}
\label{sec:results_matter}

We begin our analysis with the matter power spectrum. Our approach follows similarly to the halo Replacement analysis of \citet{Miller-25} and extends it across a systematic grid of mass-bins, radial shells, and redshifts. We describe all results within the response formalism developed in \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, thereby providing a unified account of baryonic effects induced by regions in halo mass-radial shell bins.

\subsection{Matter Power Spectrum: Cumulative Models}

For each Replace model shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:models}, we apply the Replacement procedure of \S~\ref{sec:formalism} to the 20 TNG300-1 snapshots spanning redshifts $0.03 \leq z \leq 2.56$ to generate pseudo-independent density planes of S~\ref{sec:raytracing}. For a given snapshot, particles are then assigned to two $4096^2$ pixel grids using triangle-shaped cloud (TSC) interpolation, one representing the front half and the other the rear half. We compute the overdensity field for each model $i$ and redshift $z$ as
\begin{equation}
\delta_i(\bm{x}, z) = \frac{\rho_i(\bm{x}, z) - \bar{\rho}_i(z)}{\bar{\rho}_i(z)},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\rho}_i(z)$ is the mean density of model $i$ at redshift $z$. The matter power spectrum is then computed via
\begin{equation}
P_i(k, z) = \langle |\tilde{\delta}_i(\bm{k}, z)|^2 \rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\delta}_i$ is the Fourier transform of $\delta_i$ and angle brackets denote averaging over Fourier modes in $2,047$ logarithmic $k$-bins between $k=0.037\rightarrow62.7\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ and over the pseudo-independent realizations.

We now apply the response formalism of \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, replacing the generic statistic subscript $S$ with $P$ for the matter power spectrum. We compute responses for all cumulative models represented by the $16$ models in the lower-right corner of Fig.~\ref{fig:models}—where all halos with mass $M > M_{\min}$ are Replaced within $r < \alpha R_{200}$—as well as for the discrete models shown in the upper-left corner, where both mass and radius replacement are performed in discrete bins/shells. To avoid numerical instabilities when the denominator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cum_resp_func} approaches zero, we compute response fractions $F_P(M_{\min}, \alpha; k, z)$ only at wavenumbers where baryonic suppression exceeds 1\%: $|P_{\rm H}(k,z) - P_{\rm D}(k,z)|/P_{\rm D}(k,z) > 0.01$. Additionally, all power spectra are evaluated up to the Nyquist frequency of the density grid, $k_{\rm max} = \pi N / L = 62.7\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ with $N=4096$ pixels.

Figure~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} presents a comprehensive decomposition of power spectrum suppression by mass and radius at $z=0.03$ (snapshot 96). The top row shows the power spectrum suppression, $P_{\rm R}(k)/P_{\rm D}(k)$ for cumulative Replace models compared to the full hydrodynamical target $P_{\rm H}(k)/P_{\rm D}(k)$ (black curve).

We find that even the most aggressive Replace model ($M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $\alpha=5R_{200}$) cannot fully reproduce the true hydrodynamical suppression.  Instead $\sim 90\%$ of the total hydrodynamical suppression is captured at $3 \lesssim k \lesssim 63\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$, consistent with the findings of \citet{Miller-25}, but even more jarring, as we extend the radial range used in their work from $2R_{200}$ to $5R_{200}$ and still are unable to capture the suppression fully. This implies that a non-negligible fraction of the baryonic signal impacting the power spectrum originates from halos below our mass limit ($M < 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$) and/or gas at radii beyond $5R_{200}$.

We also see that models with small radius factors exhibit spurious large-scale power enhancement, as also reported by \citet{Miller-25}. Configurations with $\alpha = 0.5$ or $1$ systematically show $P_{\rm R}/P_{\rm D} > 1$ at $k < 1\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. This counterintuitive behavior arises from mass-conservation violations: replacing only halo cores with their lower-mass hydrodynamical counterparts removes mass from high-density peaks without compensating in the outskirts, thereby artificially reducing the mean density $\bar{\rho}$ and inflating the overdensity $\delta = (\rho - \bar{\rho})/\bar{\rho}$ on large scales \citep{Miller-25}. This artifact disappears when $\alpha \geq 3$, indicating that at roughly $r\gtrsim 2R_{200}$, dark matter only and hydrodynamical halos have roughly equal masses. This is again consistent with the findings of \citet{Miller-25} and \citet{Ayromlou23}, who investigate baryon closure radii in the IllustrisTNG simulations, finding similar radii for the halos used in this work.

The second row recasts these results as cumulative response fractions $F_P(M_{\min}, \alpha; k)$. By construction, $F_P = 0$ indicates the Replace model is indistinguishable from DMO, while $F_P = 1$ implies full recovery of the hydrodynamical suppression. Values outside $[0,1]$—clearly visible in the $\alpha=0.5$ and $\alpha=1$ panels for high $M_{\min}$—directly flag mass-conservation artifacts. The cumulative responses make clear the relative importance of baryons in certain mass and radius bins, and how these effects saturate: galaxy groups and clusters with $M > 10^{13}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ Replaced out to $5R_{200}$ alone account for $F_P \sim 0.60$ (60\% of the total baryonic effects on the matter power spectrum) across $3 \lesssim k \lesssim 63\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. Including lower-mass halos down to $M_{\min} = 10^{12.5}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ increases this to $F_P \sim 0.80$ at $k \lesssim 50\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$. Further extending the mass threshold to $10^{12}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ provides additional gains, pushing $F_P$ to $\sim 0.9$ at nearly all applicable scales but never fully reaching a perfect score of $F=1.0$.

The third row explores the contributions of individual mass bin-radius shell Replace models via the discrete response of Eq.~\eqref{eq:disc_resp_func}. This decomposition shows a more detailed spatial origin of baryonic effects on the power spectrum. The cores of halos ($r < 0.5R_{200}$, leftmost panel) host the most significant effects: baryons in group-scale halos ($10^{13} \leq M\,[h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot] < 10^{13.5}$ dominate suppression at intermediate scales $3 \lesssim k \lesssim 10\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$ with $\Delta F_P^{\rm bin} \sim 0.25$, while massive clusters ($M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$) take over at more minor scales $k \gtrsim 3\,h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$.

Moving to outer shells ($0.5R_{200} < r < R_{200}$ and beyond), the contribution pattern shifts: galaxy clusters maintain a nearly constant $\Delta F_P^{\rm bin} \sim 0.05$–$0.1$ across all outer radial bins, consistent with AGN feedback redistributing gas uniformly to $r \sim 2$–$3R_{200}$ in massive systems \citep{Ayromlou23}. Lower-mass halos ($M < 10^{13}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$) exhibit weaker radial dependence overall.

The bottom row quantifies the degree of non-linearity in the response using Eq.~\eqref{eq:non_additivity}. Values near $\epsilon = 0$ indicate that discrete bins sum linearly to reproduce cumulative models while deviations signal non-additive coupling in mass radius space. While most configurations exhibit relatively good additivity ($|\epsilon| \lesssim 0.05$), the highest radius factor $\alpha = 5$ shows significant non-linearity for all mass thresholds, with low-mass halos displaying $|\epsilon| \sim 0.1$–$0.2$. This breakdown likely arises from contamination by the two-halo term. At large radii ($r \gtrsim 3R_{200}$), the Replacement models begin to include the correlated environments of neighboring halos during the Replacement procedure, altering the two-halo term.  Replacing the outskirts of one halo then affects not only its own contribution to $P(k)$ but also its correlation with nearby halos, breaking additivity.

To synthesize the scale-dependent information in Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} into a single metric per model, we compute the SNR-weighted mean fractional response of Eq.\ref{eq:weighted_mean_response}. Figure~\ref{fig:response} presents $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ at $z=0.03$ as a heatmap over the model space in mass and radius and provides a quick-reference guide to the results of Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction}. To capture $\sim 90\%$ of the baryonic effects on the power spectrum ($\langle F_P \rangle_k \gtrsim 0.9$), one must correctly reconstruct the density distribution of \emph{all} halos above $10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to at least $5R_{200}$ (bottom right most panel). More modest goals can be met with less aggressive requirements, such as $\sim65\%$ if only considering massive halos with $M > 10^{13} h^{-1} {\rm M}_\odot$.

The upper left corner of the figure shows responses for discrete mass-radius shells, revealing the detailed spatial origin of the $P(k)$ suppression. The brightest cell (dark red, $\langle \Delta F_P^{\rm bin} \rangle_k \sim 0.22$) appears at $(M, r) = ([10^{13.5}, \infty]\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot, [0, 0.5R_{200}))$, showing that the majority of baryonic effects on the power spectrum occur in cluster-scale halo cores. The regions between the core and the virial radius show relatively little sensitivity to the baryonic modifications. Still, the exterior shells ($r \in [3, 5]R_{200}$) show a resurgence to $\langle \Delta F_P^{\rm bin} \rangle_k \sim 0.05$–$0.1$ for all mass-bins. This non-monotonic radial profile reflects extended gas redistribution plus two-halo correlations. Because the mass and radial bins are approximately independent (except at $\alpha = 5$ where non-additivity emerges, Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} bottom row), summing across rows or down columns of the right panel roughly reconstructs the cumulative heatmap on the left, providing a useful consistency check.

It is important to caution the reader’s interpretation of Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} and Fig.~\ref{fig:response}. First, while the cores of massive halos dominate the response—with the $\alpha\in[0,0.5]$, $M>10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ shell alone contributing $\Delta F_P \sim 0.22$, we cannot conclude that precisely modeling cores is sufficient for reproducing the matter power spectrum. The remaining shells jointly contribute $\sim 70\%$ of the response. The cores are necessary but not sufficient.

More fundamentally, it is essential now to distinguish between where baryonic feedback physically acts and the spatial regions a statistic is sensitive to in the presence of baryons. Our response functions measure the latter, not the former. The right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:response} does not imply that $22\%$ of the baryonic mass redistribution occurs within halo cores. Instead, it indicates that $22\%$ of the statistical signature on $P(k)$ originates from differences in the core density profiles between hydrodynamical and dark-matter-only runs.

For observables that probe the matter distribution across cosmic time, such as weak gravitational lensing, understanding the redshift dependence of baryonic effects on statistics is crucial. Weak lensing, in particular, represents a projected matter density along a line of sight from an observer to redshift $z$, highlighting the importance of understanding baryonic signatures in statistics as a function of redshift.

Figure~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution} shows the evolution of $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ from $z=2.56$ to $z=0.03$ for all cumulative (top panels) and discrete (bottom panels) models. One noticeable effect is that baryonic imprints on statistics strengthen toward low redshift for all except the lowest mass threshold. This reflects the growth of feedback-driven modifications as halo assembly proceeds and AGN/stellar feedback intensify. The steepest evolution occurs between $z \sim 2$ and $z \sim 0.3$, coinciding with the epoch of peak star formation and black-hole accretion in the TNG model \citep{}.

The bottom panels decompose this evolution into discrete mass-radius shells. Halo cores ($r < 0.5R_{200}$, leftmost bottom panel) show the most substantial baryonic imprint evolution. Crucially, there is a mass-dependent inversion, where at lower redshift ($z \lesssim 0.5$), massive clusters ($M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$) dominate the core response, whereas at higher redshift ($z \gtrsim 0.5$), lower-mass halos ($M \sim 10^{12}$–$10^{12.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$) become more critical, surpassing clusters.

In outer shells ($r > 0.5R_{200}$), the redshift evolution weakens considerably. Contributions from $0.5R_{200} < r < R_{200}$ and $R_{200} < r < 3R_{200}$ remain roughly constant at $\langle \Delta F_P^{\rm bin} \rangle_k \sim 0.05$ across all redshifts. The weak redshift evolution of outer shells has important implications for weak lensing observables. The rapidly evolving core signal should produce detectable redshift-dependent features in lensing statistics, particularly for observables sensitive to both high and low-mass halos. \section{The Baryonic Response of Weak Lensing Observables}
\label{sec:results_WL}
The previous section demonstrated the response of the matter power spectrum to baryonic effects, replicating and extending the halo Replacement analysis of \citet{Miller-25} using the response formalism of \S~\ref{sec:formalism}. This allowed a systematic quantification of the importance of baryon physics across halo mass and radius at various redshifts. We now apply the same framework to weak gravitational lensing statistics.

Following the $\kappa$TNG ray-tracing procedure described in \S~\ref{sec:raytracing}, we generate 2,000 pseudo-independent convergence maps for each of the $ 64$ Replace models shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:models}. Each map covers a $5\deg \times 5\deg$ field of view at pixel resolution $0.29\,{\rm arcmin}$ (corresponding to $1024^2$ pixels) and source redshifts spanning $z_s = 0.034$ to $z_s = 2.57$ using the 40 lens plane redshifts listed in Table~1 of \citet{Osato21}. This yields a total of $5.28$ million convergence maps, $\kappa$, across all models and 40 source redshifts. We make this dataset publicly available for community use and exploration.\footnote{Data release details and access instructions are available at columbialensing.org}

We begin with the Gaussian statistics of the weak lensing field: the angular power spectrum. For each convergence map, we compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C_l}
C_{\kappa}(\ell_i) = \langle|\tilde{\kappa}(\bm{\ell})|^2\rangle_{\bm{\ell} \in [\ell_i^{\rm min}, \ell_i^{\rm max}]},
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\kappa}(\bm{\ell})$ is the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the convergence field $\kappa(\bm{\theta})$, and the angle brackets denote averaging over all Fourier modes $\bm{\ell}$ within the multipole bin $[\ell_i^{\rm min}, \ell_i^{\rm max}]$. This yields angular power spectra for each of the 2,000 pseudo-independent maps across all Replace models and source redshifts. We compute the same quantities for the non-Replace models (i.e., pure TNG-hydro and TNG-DMO), allowing us to define the cumulative response fraction for the convergence power spectrum following Eq.~\ref{eq:cum_resp_func}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F_cl}
F_{C_\ell}(M_{\min}, \alpha; \ell, z_s) = \frac{\langle C_\ell^{\rm R}(M_{\min}, \alpha; z_s) \rangle - \langle C_\ell^{\rm DMO}(z_s) \rangle}{\langle C_\ell^{\rm H}(z_s) \rangle - \langle C_\ell^{\rm DMO}(z_s) \rangle},
\end{equation}
where the angle brackets denote averaging the measured statistic over the 2,000 map realizations.

Figure~\ref{fig:C_ell_scale_dependent_response_z1} is analogous to Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} and shows the convergence power spectrum suppression (top row), cumulative response fractions (second row), discrete response fractions (third row), and non-additivity statistic (fourth row) at source redshift $z_s = 1.03$. The black curve in the top row shows the complete hydrodynamical suppression $C_\ell^{\rm H}/C_\ell^{\rm DMO}$.

Similar to the matter power spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction}), the most aggressive Replacement—all halos with $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$ almost entirely reproduces the hydrodynamical suppression across all multipoles but not fully, achieving $F_{C_\ell} \gtrsim 0.95$ at $10^4\lesssim \ell \lesssim 3\times10^5$. However, there is a steep fall off in $F_{C_\ell}$ at smaller scales at high significance.

Models with small radius factors ($\alpha = 0.5$ or $1$) and low mass show suppression at large scales ($\ell \lesssim 500$), causing $C_\ell^{\rm R}/C_\ell^{\rm DMO}$ to exceed the bounds of $0$ and $1$. This also occurs in regions where the true baryonic effect is already quite small, and the values of $F$ are already masked in the analysis. This mass conservation artifact is qualitatively inverse to that seen in the matter power spectrum, but appears at lower $\ell$ (larger angular scales).

In the third row of Fig.~\ref{fig:C_ell_scale_dependent_response_z1}, we show the discrete response for the mass and radial bins. Similar to the matter power spectrum, we see the dominant impact from galaxy groups of $10^{13} -10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$. But, unlike the matter power spectrum, this mass regime and in the centers of halos appears to dominate across virtually all $\ell$. While galaxy clusters increase in their response to baryons at smaller scales, they never outweigh the influence of the galaxy groups. interestingly, at the smallest scales, the lower mass $(10^{12.5}-10^{13}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot)$ halos begin to dominate the response. Similarly, outside of the halo core, the lower mass halos dominate the baryonic response out to $R{200}$. Though for the exteriors of halos $(3-5R_{200})$, baryonic effects on $C_\ell$ are seemingly purely sourced by clusters, causing an overall 10\% increase on large to intermediate scales.

The final column of the figure shows the non-linearity statistic of Eq.~\ref{eq:non_additivity}. We see that up to $R_{200}$, all mass-bins are approximately additive and linear. But outside of this, the lower mass halos become quite non-linear, reaching a maximum of $\epsilon\sim0.3$ for the lowest mass-bins. The galaxy clusters remain relatively linear, but all other mass-bins show a similar trend towards nonlinearity as the radius factor increases. This is likely explained by the same reasoning as provided for Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} for the power spectrum; we are observing sensitivities to modifications in the two-halo term when applying Replacements so far outside of the halo. The key differences in the $C_\ell$ and $P(k)$ statistics are that the weak lensing statistic integrates the effects of baryons over various redshifts, weighted by a lensing kernel. So while we see a comparable structure, the baryonic imprints are fundamentally different.

Figure~\ref{fig:C_ell_cumulative_summary_z1} condenses this scale-dependent information into the multipole-averaged response fraction $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell$, computed analogously to $\langle F_P \rangle_k$ with Eq.~\ref{eq:weighted_mean_response}. We average over all multipoles where $|C_\ell^{\rm H} - C_\ell^{\rm DMO}|/C_\ell^{\rm DMO} > 0.01$ and weigh each bin by the relative importance computed by the DMO to hydrodynamical differences and the scatter across the $2000$ realizations. The left panel shows that lowering the mass threshold from $10^{13}$ to $10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ at fixed $\alpha = 1$ increases $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell$ from $\sim 0.51$ to $\sim 0.77$—a dramatic gain indicating that low-mass halos cannot be ignored in their importance to the angular power spectrum. Similarly, extending the radius from $R_{200}$ to $3R_{200}$ yields only modest improvement.

Replacing halos with $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $R_{200}$ (i.e., $\alpha = 1$) already captures $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell \sim 0.84$—approximately 84\% of the baryonic effect on the angular power spectrum at $z_s \sim 1$. To achieve $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell \gtrsim 0.92$, one must extend to at least $5R_{200}$ for halos above $10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$. This is a very similar requirement to that for the matter power spectrum, which required $5R_{200}$ to approach 91\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:response}).

The right panel shows the discrete models in a similar way to Fig.~\ref{fig:response}. One of the key differences we see between the matter power spectrum and the angular power spectrum is that the angular power spectrum is less sensitive to galaxy clusters and more sensitive to galaxy groups and lower-mass groups. We also see that the baryons in the exteriors of the halos have a larger influence on $C_\ell$ than on $P(k)$ which can naturally be explained by the fact that in lensing, the halo cores are diluted by the projections along the line of sight. 

Finally, Fig.~\ref{fig:Cl_z_evolution} extends this analysis across source redshifts from $z_s = 0.03$ to $z_s = 2.57$. The layout mirrors Fig.~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution}, with each panel showing $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle_\ell$ as a function of source redshift for fixed $\alpha$ and varying $M_{\min}$ in the top row and for discrete bins in the bottom row.

The convergence power spectrum response shows much weaker redshift dependence than the matter power spectrum. Unlike the matter power spectrum, the convergence power spectrum response does not show a clear signature of low-mass halos “turning off” their feedback at high redshift. This is again due to projection effects: even though individual low-mass halos at $z \sim 2$ may exhibit weak feedback effects on the power spectrum (as seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution}), the lensing signal for high-$z_s$ sources is dominated by galaxy groups where feedback is more pronounced.

These results demonstrate that precisely modeling baryonic effects on the weak lensing convergence power spectrum for Stage-III and Stage-IV surveys requires capturing halos down to at least $M \sim 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, and extending halo modifications to at least $3R_{200}$, and preferably capturing two-halo and nonlinear effects from $5R_{200}$.

\subsection{Beyond Gaussian statistics}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{imgs/peaks_response_all_z1.pdf}
\caption{Response of convergence peak counts to baryonic effects as a function of signal-to-noise ratio $\nu$ at source redshift $z_s \approx 1.0$ following the same structure as Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} and Fig.~\ref{fig:C_ell_cumulative_summary_z1}}
\label{fig:peaks_by_radius}
\end{figure}

We now extend our response analysis to non-Gaussian statistics of the weak lensing convergence field, which probe the matter distribution in qualitatively different ways than the power spectrum. We focus on four complementary statistics computed using the \emph{LensTools} package \citep{Petri16a}\footnote{\url{lenstools.readthedocs.io}} from the convergence maps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Peak counts} $N_p(\nu)$: The number of local maxima in the convergence field, defined as pixels with $\nu$ with greater values than their $8$ nearest neighbors, as a function of signal-to-noise ratio $\nu = \kappa/\sigma_\kappa$, where $\sigma_\kappa = \sqrt{\langle \kappa^2 \rangle}$ is the root-mean-square convergence \citep{}.
\item \textbf{Minimum counts} $N_{\rm m}(\nu)$: The number of local minima in the convergence field with $\nu$ values smaller than their $8$-nearest neighbors \citep{}.
\item \textbf{One-point PDF} $p(\nu)$: The probability distribution of convergence values representing the full histogram of $\nu$ values \citep{}.
\item \textbf{Minkowski functionals} (MFs): Three descriptors of convergence excursion sets \citep{}—area $V_0(\nu)$, boundary length $V_1(\nu)$, and genus $V_2(\nu)$—that encode shape information.
\end{enumerate}

These statistics complement the angular power spectrum by accessing non-Gaussian features of the matter distribution, which have been shown to hold a wealth of cosmological information \cite{}. For brevity, we present detailed results for peak counts in the main text; analogous analyses for minima, the PDF, and Minkowski functionals are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_nongaussian}.

For each of the $5.28$ million convergence maps, we compute these statistics on the unsmoothed, noise-free convergence field. We bin peak and minima counts in 50 linearly spaced $\nu$ bins spanning $-5 \leq \nu \leq 10$. The cumulative and discrete response fractions are defined analogously to the matter and angular power spectrum,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:F_peak}
F_{\rm peak}(M_{\min}, \alpha; \nu, z_s) = \frac{\langle N_{\rm R}(\nu; M_{\min}, \alpha, z_s) \rangle - \langle N_{\rm D}(\nu; z_s) \rangle}{\langle N_{\rm H}(\nu; z_s) \rangle - \langle N_{\rm D}(\nu; z_s) \rangle},
\end{equation}
where $N_{\rm R}$, $N_{\rm D}$, and $N_{\rm H}$ are the peak counts in Replace, DMO, and hydrodynamical maps, respectively, and angle brackets denote averaging over the 2,000 map realizations in each case.

Figure~\ref{fig:peaks_by_radius} shows the cumulative response of peak counts to baryonic effects as a function of $\nu$ at source redshift $z_s \approx 1.0$ paralleling the displays of Fig.~\ref{fig:suppression_and_fraction} and Fig.~\ref{fig:C_ell_cumulative_summary_z1}. As in the previous sections, we again see that baryonic effects on peak counts are primarily sourced by halo cores. The leftmost panel ($\alpha = 0.5$) shows that replacing only the innermost $0.5R_{200}$ of halos achieves $F_{\rm peak} \sim 0.6$–$0.8$ for high-$\nu$ peaks ($\nu \gtrsim 4$), capturing 60–80\% of the total baryonic effect. High-$\nu$ convergence peaks are thought to be sourced by individual massive halos whose lensing signal is determined primarily by the projected density within $\sim R_{200}$ \citep{Yang-13, liu16} and baryonic processes that contract or expand the core density profile, such as AGN feedback evacuating gas from cluster centers or stellar feedback redistributing baryons in groups.

Baryonic effects on peak statistics also appear to saturate at smaller radii than the power spectrum. By $\alpha = 3$ (third panel), $F_{\rm peak} \gtrsim 0.95$ across nearly all $\nu$, indicating that baryonic modifications beyond $3R_{200}$ contribute negligibly to peak counts. Extending to $\alpha = 5$ (the fourth panel) yields a marginal improvement. This contrasts with the power spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:response} and Fig.~\ref{fig:C_ell_scale_dependent_response_z1}), which required $\alpha = 5$ to approach $\sim90\%$ fidelity. The difference arises because peak counts probe the local density contrast around individual halos. In contrast, the power spectrum integrates over all Fourier modes, including those sensitive to large-scale correlations between halos and their extended outskirts.

In the discrete view of peaks in the third row, the innermost core region $0 \leq r < 0.5R_{200}$, exhibits clear mass structure in $\nu$-space: low-mass halos ($10^{12}$–$10^{13}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, purple/blue curves) dominate the response at $\nu \sim 2.5$–$4$, groups ($10^{13}$–$10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, yellow) control $\nu \sim 5$–$8$, and clusters ($M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, red) dominate the highest peaks $\nu \gtrsim 8$.

The middle and right panels reveal a qualitatively different behavior for halo outskirts ($r > 0.5R_{200}$): the discrete response $\Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin}$ is approximately constant in $\nu$ at $\sim 0.05$ for all radial shells beyond $R_{200}$. This indicates that gas redistribution in halo outskirts produces a broad, nearly uniform effect on peak counts across all $\nu$, rather than selectively influencing specific peak heights. The most significant effect comes from cluster outskirts ($M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, red curves in second from the right panel), where AGN-driven gas redistribution at $1R_{200} < r < 3R_{200}$ contributes $\Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \sim 0.1$ uniformly.

Figure~\ref{fig:peaks_redshift_evolution}  shows the redshift evolution of the baryonic impact on peaks analogous to Figs.~\ref{fig:power_spectrum_z_evolution} and~\ref{fig:Cl_z_evolution}. The left panels ($0 \leq r < 0.5R_{200}$) show that at low source redshifts ($z_s \lesssim 0.5$), low-mass halos dominate the peak response, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu \sim 0.4$ for $10^{12.5}$–$10^{13}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$. Conversely, at high source redshifts ($z_s \gtrsim 1.5$), only more massive halos contribute, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu \sim 0.2$ for $M > 10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ and $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu \sim 0.35$ for the intermediate and more massive groups $10^{12.5}$–$10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$. Outer radial shells ($r > 0.5R_{200}$) exhibit much weaker redshift evolution, with $\langle \Delta F_{\rm peak}^{\rm bin} \rangle_\nu$ remaining roughly constant at $\sim 0.05$–$0.15$ across all $z_s$. This insensitivity to source redshift indicates that gas at $r \sim R_{200}$–$5R_{200}$ contributes a smooth convergence signal that uniformly boosts the peak counts. In all peak count analyses, halo cores dominate the response to baryonic effects, but do not fully capture them. For that, we would require correct modifications out to $3R_{200}$. 

One can use the above information to cross-validate BCMs. The distinct $\nu$-$M$ structure (third row of Fig.~\ref{fig:peaks_by_radius}, left panel) means that BCMs can be tested by checking whether they reproduce the correct peak counts at different $\nu$ thresholds. A model that fits high-$\nu$ peaks ($\nu \gtrsim 8$, cluster-dominated) but fails at $\nu \sim 3$–$5$ (group-dominated) reveals inconsistent treatment of feedback across mass scales.

In Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_nongaussian}, we present response analyses analogous to peaks for minimum counts, the one-point PDF, and Minkowski functionals. Here, we synthesize these results in a comparative heatmap that summarizes the main findings of this section. Figure~\ref{fig:all_Statistics_heatmap} compares the weighted mean response $\langle F_S(M_{\min}, \alpha) \rangle$ across eight statistics at source redshift $z_s \approx 1.0$ following the structure of Fig.~\ref{eq:cum_resp_func} and Fig.~\ref{fig:C_ell_cumulative_summary_z1}. We see that even the most aggressive halo Replacement fails to reproduce baryonic effects in most statistics fully. The bottom-right cells of each heatmap—corresponding to replacing all halos with $M > 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$—achieve $\langle F_S \rangle =1.0$ only for peak counts.

Statistics that exhibit this failure to reach $F_S = 1$ even with perfect field level Replacements suggest physical mechanisms beyond our modeling framework. Possibilities include: (1) baryonic effects in halos below our mass threshold ($M < 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$). These mass ranges were excluded from this work primarily because low-mass halos are not included in standard baryon-correction models. This is typically the case because the physically motivated BCM parameters can be fit to X-ray and SZ observations, but this limites them to higher-mass halos. (2) The diffuse intergalactic medium (IGM), such as gas in filaments or galactic outflows extending beyond 5 times the virial radii of halos. And (3) the non-additivity and inter-halo coupling, where mass redistribution between halo cores and outskirts cannot be separated into independent contributions. The fact that peak counts do reach $F_{\rm peak} \approx 1$ suggests they are uniquely insensitive to these diffuse components, consistent with their status as local, halo-centric statistics \citep{Yang-13, liu16}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:all_Statistics_heatmap} also represents an essential point for BCM validation. Namely, any baryon model that fits the statistics presented here to sub-percent precision must, by construction, violate field-level accuracy at the percent level. For example, a BCM calibrated to match the observed convergence power spectrum to $\Delta C_\ell / C_\ell < 1\%$ cannot simultaneously place field-level baryonic effects in the correct locations within/outside of halos, because even our Replace procedure (which uses the accurate hydrodynamical field-level distribution) falls short by $\sim 8\%$. This means that at the percent level, BCMs should be interpreted as phenomenological models that reproduce summary statistics, rather than as an accurate physical representation of baryonic effects. BCMs fit to a given statistic such as $P(k)$ then make two mistakes which cancel each other: they over fit the effect on halos and ignore the effects outside of their radial influence or below their mass threshold. This distinction becomes critical when combining multiple observables: a BCM tuned to $C_\ell$ will likely predict incorrect peak counts, or minima, if those statistics weight baryonic effects differently across halo masses and radial shells.

Fig.~\ref{fig:all_Statistics_heatmap} also shows that different statistics exhibit different saturation scales in the $M-R$ space. Peak counts achieve $\langle F_{\rm peak} \rangle =1$ by $\alpha = 3$ for $M_{\min} = 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, whereas the convergence power spectrum requires extension to $\alpha = 5$ to reach $\langle F_{C_\ell} \rangle \sim 0.95$. Minimum counts fall between these extremes, saturating around $\alpha = 3$–$5$ with $\langle F_{\rm min} \rangle \sim 0.69$ for $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$. The power spectrum shows the most gradual saturation, with visible color gradients persisting across $\alpha$ values.

In the bottom panel, we again see that baryonic effects in cores source the majority of the impact on most statistics. However, the level of importance and mass scales of importance differ between statistics. Minimum counts show enhanced sensitivity to low-mass halo cores compared to peaks, while Minkowski functionals exhibit the most significant sensitivity to the massive galaxy clusters.

\section{Baryon correction model consistency tests}
\label{sec:BCM}
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{imgs/bcm_vs_Replace_F_comparison.pdf}
    \caption{Response fraction comparison between the Arico+2020 BCM and cumulative Replace models for the matter power spectrum at $z\sim0.96$. \emph{Top:} The \citet{Arico2020} model (gray) successfully reproduces the target hydrodynamical suppression (black) at percent-level accuracy by design. \emph{Bottom:} The BCM's response fraction $F_P^{\rm BCM}(k)$ (gray) tracks between cumulative Replace models, sitting closest to Replace $M>10^{13}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ at intermediate scales ($k\sim3$--10 $h\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}$) and approaching $M>10^{12}$ at smaller scales ($k\gtrsim10$).}
    \label{fig:F_BCM}
\end{figure}

The previous sections have provided a detailed overview of how baryons affect various statistics,  from the full 3D density field to non-Gaussian summary statistics. We now briefly consider how baryonic correction models (BCMs), which apply spherical particle displacements, may influence statistics and are able to capture the underlying physical baryonic effects.

BCMs have been shown to reproduce summary statistics such as the matter power spectrum at the few-percent level \citep{Arico2020}, and recent work has demonstrated simultaneous fits to multiple two-point statistics \citep{aricoSimultaneousModellingMatter2021, Grandis-24}. However, \citet{leeComparingWeakLensing2022} found that the highest significance peaks $\nu\gtrsim4$ were not suppressed enough compared to the true hydrodynamical simulations when using the \citet{Arico2020} BCM fit to the matter power spectrum. Here, we use the response fingerprints from earlier to hypothesize why that could be the case.

Figure~\ref{fig:F_BCM} shows the matter power spectrum suppression from IllustrisTNG at $z\sim0.96$ alongside the \citet{Arico2020} best-fit model (applied to all TNG-DM halos with $M>2\times10^{12},h^{-1},{\rm M}_\odot$ following the original implementation of \citet{Arico2020}. The top panel confirms percent-level agreement between BCM and target hydro suppression by as expected.

The bottom panel presents the response following the methods in of \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, comparing $F_P^{\rm BCM}(k)$ to cumulative Replace models. The BCM achieves $F_P \sim 0.8$ across $k\gtrsim 3$, closely tracking the Replace $M>10^{13}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$, $\alpha=5$ model at intermediate scales and approaching $M>10^{12.5}$, $\alpha=5$ at smaller scales. This is consistent with the expectation that the BCM modifies halos down to $M\sim2\times10^{12},h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, capturing baryonic contributions to the power spectrum from the mass range where our fingerprint analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:all_Statistics_heatmap}) shows $P(k)$ to be sensitive.

However, matching the $P(k)$ response does not guarantee correct field-level baryon placement within individual halos or matching individual power spectra. The BCM could achieve $F_P^{\rm BCM} \sim 0.8$ by compensating between regions in $(M,r)$ space that are degenerate for $P(k)$ but distinguishable by other statistics.

To test this directly, we compare density profiles from several widely used BCMs \citep{Schneider19, Arico2020, Schneider-25} to bijectively matched TNG profiles. For each model, we apply the standard BCM displacement prescription using the BaryonForge\footnote{\url{https://baryonforge.readthedocs.io/en/latest/}} package \citep{Anbajagane2024bcm} to every bijectively matched halo in the TNG-DM catalog and compute the resulting density profile as a function of radius. Figure~\ref{fig:bcm_vs_hydro} shows the ratio of BCM-to-Hydro and DMO-to-DMO hydro, averaged in the same mass-bins used in the previous sections of this work.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{imgs/BCM_individual_profiles_by_mass.pdf}
\caption{Comparison of bijectively matched density profiles from various BCMs shown as ratios to their hydrodynamical counterpart. Each panel shows a halo mass-bin, with the solid black curves showing the DMO/TNG hydrodynamical ratio. Colored curves show BCM/DMO ratios for: \citep{Schneider19} (blue), \cite{Arico2020} (green), and \cite{Schneider-25} (orange). All BCMs exhibit under-concentration in cores ($r \lesssim 0.5,R_{200}$), though the innermost radial bins of the halos appear to be over-concentrated for the Schneider model. We first see that all BCMs exhibit under-concentration in most of the halo core regions ($r \lesssim 0.5,R_{200}$), with BCM/Hydro ratios systematically below unity. In the innermost bins, the \citet{Schneider19} model shows a significant over-concentration, but the ensuing under-prediction still outweighs this. We also see in massive clusters ($M \gtrsim 10^{13.5} h^{-1} {\rm M}_\odot$) that BCMs systematically overextend the baryon expulsion, showing a noticeable peak at $2R_{200}$. In contrast, the true hydrodynamical profiles converge to the dark matter-only densities by this distance.

Among the models tested, \citet{Arico2020} shows the closest overall agreement, which is unsurprising given that it is the only model that was fit discretely to the IllustrisTNG power spectrum. But this is a nuanced point. While a fit to the power spectrum does not guarantee a match at the profile or field level, it yields a better fit to the density profile than a model not directly tuned to the IllustrisTNG power spectrum. But even this parameterization exhibits large residuals in cores and outer shells. To highlight this, we show the integrated mass for each profile for bijectively matched BCM and hydro halos and their residual in Fig.~\ref{fig:shell_mass_comparison}. The top panel shows each halo’s integrated mass, while the bottom plot shows the residual with respect to the 1-to-1 line clearly showing that for low-mass halos, the model underpredicts core concentration by up to 20\%.

Taken together with Fig.~\ref{fig:all_Statistics_heatmap}, this is a clear explanation for why \citet{leeComparingWeakLensing2022} found discrepancies at the level of peak counts. Weak lensing peaks are particularly sensitive to core regions of $10^{12.5}-10^{13.5}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$, and we can clearly see that below $10^{13}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, the BCM model fails to reproduce the cores of these halos, and instead induces a suppression. This systematic suppression leads to discrepancies for peak counts.

More generally, a BCM that fits one statistic can trade off baryonic effects between $(M,\alpha)$ regions that are degenerate for that statistic but distinguishable by others. For example, fitting to the peaks may induce degeneracies between the $10^{12.5} - 10^{13}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ mass-bins. On the other hand, the Minkowski functional $V_1$ is primarily determined by the higher-mass halos and could break this degeneracy.

We want to highlight an important caveat: our Replace formalism provides an optimistic benchmark for BCMs. Even if a hypothetical BCM perfectly reproduced the spherically averaged density profiles of all halos (eliminating the discrepancies in Fig.~\ref{fig:bcm_vs_hydro}), it would still potentially fail to fully recover hydrodynamical statistics, for two reasons.

First, our cumulative Replace models show that even replacing all matched halos with $M \geq 10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $5,R_{200}$ captures only $\lesssim 90$\% of the hydrodynamical suppression in $P(k)$ and $C_\ell$ (Figs.~\ref{fig:all_Statistics_heatmap}. BCMs, which operate only on halos, cannot capture extragalactic or low mass components without additional modeling.

Second, BCMs impose spherical symmetry when applying radial displacements, whereas true hydrodynamical halos are far more complex, showing, for example, triaxiality and large-scale alignments of outflows with filaments. Our Replace fields preserve the complete three-dimensional structure of the hydrodynamical simulation within activated shells. The additional error introduced by spherical averaging is challenging to quantify without running a full BCM through our response pipeline, which we leave to future work.

These results, therefore, represent an upper bound. Even a perfectly spherical BCM calibrated to TNG would fall short of the complete Replace benchmark, and statistics sensitive to non-spherical structure would see larger discrepancies.

X-ray and SZ observations of group and cluster gas can externally constrain BCM parameters. Our kernels provide complementary information; mass-bins and radii must be modeled accurately to achieve the target precision for each cosmological observable. It is clear that, for virtually all statistics, fitting only to observables from high-mass halos is just not enough. We must incorporate BCM parameters applicable to the lower-mass regimes.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, we have introduced a framework for quantifying the origins of baryonic effects on various large-scale-structure statistics. We construct hybrid Replace fields that selectively inject hydrodynamical particles to replace dark matter in dark-matter-only simulations across a grid of halo-mass bins and radial shells. From this, we empirically mapped the statistical response to baryons across the 2D matter and 2D angular weak-lensing convergence power spectra, weak-lensing peak counts, minima, the one-point PDF, and Minkowski functionals. Our key findings are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Incomplete field-level recovery of baryonic effects sets fundamental limits on statistics. Even replacing all halos above $10^{12}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ out to $5R_{200}$ captures only $\sim90\%$ of the hydrodynamical suppression in $P(k)$ and $C_\ell$. The remaining $\sim10\%$ originates from lower-mass halos, radii beyond $5R_{200}$, or diffuse intergalactic components, establishing an upper bound on what halo-based correction schemes can achieve.

\item Baryons have a distinct fingerprint on each statistic. The matter power spectrum responds to baryons across halo masses and radial shells, while the effect on peak counts is sharply localized to halo cores ($M\gtrsim10^{12.5}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$, $r < R_{200}$). Minima show systematically weaker responses across all Replace models, reflecting their sensitivity to underdense regions largely unaffected by halo-centric modifications. These fingerprints uniquely encode which models accurately predict baryon responses for each statistic.

\item Baryon correction models exhibit systematic spatial biases. When applied to bijectively matched TNG halos, existing BCMs calibrated to $P(k)$ under-predict core masses by $\sim20\%$ for halos below $10^{13}\,h^{-1}{\rm M}_\odot$ while over-extending baryon expulsion in cluster outskirts. Because peak counts are sensitive to baryonic contributions at these core regions, BCMs tuned to $P(k)$ systematically fail for peak statistics. This explains discrepancies reported in prior work \citep{leeComparingWeakLensing2022}. More generally, fitting one statistic may allow BCMs to trade baryonic effects between $(M, r)$ regions that are degenerate for that observable but distinguishable by others, i.e. two mistakes made by BCMs can cancel when fit to a given statistic but can be noticible for other statistics.
\end{enumerate}

Achieving the percent-level systematic control required by Euclid, LSST, and Roman demands either hydrodynamical simulations covering the whole cosmological and subgrid astrophysical parameter space, or models of baryonic effects that can be validated independently against multiple observables with distinct mass-radius sensitivities. Our baryonic fingerprint analysis pinpoints which halo populations must be modeled most accurately for each science case, enabling targeted calibration strategies.

In the future, we hope to apply this framework to alternative hydrodynamical suites (FLAMINGO, SIMBA, EAGLE) to assess subgrid-model dependencies, to BCMs to explore more rigorously their placement of baryons in halo mass and radial shell space, and to construct data-driven emulators that interpolate baryonic effects across the full $(M, r)$ space. The baryonic response formalism provides both a diagnostic tool for existing models and a roadmap for next-generation correction schemes that must simultaneously satisfy the diverse requirements of multiple cosmological probes.

\section{Replacement cuttoff scale effects}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{imgs/ramp_test_alpha0.5_power.pdf}
\caption{Difference in matter power spectrum between hard boundary and smooth cosine ramp Replacement methods for halos with $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ at $\alpha=0.5R_{200}$. The smooth ramping (with $\delta=0.1R_{200}$) produces sub-percent level differences across all scales used in the analysis, justifying the hard boundary approach adopted throughout this work.}
\label{fig:ramp_test}
\end{figure}
One potential concern is the boundary effects induced by the Replacement methods outlined in \S~\ref{sec:formalism}, as we enforce hard boundaries at the various $\alpha$ levels. To determine whether this will significantly affect our analysis, we perform a simple test. We apply a Replacement for all halos with $M>10^{12}\,h^{-1}\,{\rm M}_\odot$ within $\alpha=0.5R_{200}$. We perform the same Replacement, but instead of a hard boundary, we apply a smooth ramp between dark matter-only and hydrodynamical particles by weighting particles with a cosine ramp.
\begin{equation}
w® = \begin{cases}
0 & r < \alpha R_{200} - \delta \
\frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \cos\left(\pi\frac{r - \alpha R_{200}}{\delta}\right)\right] & \alpha R_{200} - \delta \leq r \leq \alpha R_{200} + \delta \
1 & r > \alpha R_{200} + \delta
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where (w=0) corresponding to the hydrodynamic simulation particles and (w=1) to the dark matter-only particles, with $\delta=0.1R_{200}$ providing the transition width for each halo.

We measure the matter power spectrum for both fields and compare the effects. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ramp_test}, applying smoothing rather than a rigid boundary results in sub-percent changes in the power spectrum across all scales. At the Nyquist frequency, the impacts become somewhat larger, but these scales are removed from all analyses presented in this work. This validates the usage of a Replacement scheme with rigid boundaries as implemented throughout the main text.
